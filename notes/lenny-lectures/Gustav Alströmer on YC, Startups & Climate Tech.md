## 🏠 What Made Airbnb So Special

### The Core Ingredients
- *"It did not feel like a normal job. It felt more like a group of friends trying to just do something together."*
- Not career people from the technology industry — many were former founders
- Diverse backgrounds created a different decision-making culture
- The first 10-15 PMs were mostly former founders — set the bar for everything after

### The Hiring Philosophy
1. **Genuine excitement**: People who really wanted to build Airbnb specifically
2. **Culture interviews**: Deep dives to understand true motivations and alignment with core values
3. **Diverse backgrounds**: Former founders, not just career tech people

### The Business Model Advantage
> *"It's hard to fail with a company you can fail with individual things inside the company but a company was still going to succeed."*

- Having an obviously great business gave people the confidence to take risks
- Without that foundation, even great culture can't save a company
- The Airbnb alumni bond: *"It reminds me of close social connections more than anything else — not co-workers"*

## 💡 Why People Start Companies: The Motivation Question

### The YC Office Hours Question
*"Why are you doing this? Why will you come in and work late after four years when you have no money left?"*

- The "niche market" answer is NOT the right answer
- There's something deeper — the motivation that sustains you through the hard times

### The Diversity of Valid Motivations
- Solving a technical problem they're passionate about
- Proving themselves to others or to themselves
- Grand missions: give everyone water, solve climate change, democratize publishing
- Just wanting to build a big successful company

> *"In my experience, it doesn't matter what your motivation is. I don't think either of these motivations is better than others."*

### Motivations Change Over Time
- Eventually, just running the company becomes the motivation
- The day-to-day of building something big becomes its own reward
- Not everything has to be shiny and grandiose

### When to Discourage Starting a Company
- If you think of it as a career step → it is NOT a career step
- If successful: it'll be your entire career for 10+ years
- If there are financial, family, or relationship constraints that will trump the startup
- *"Startups are much harder than a normal job — equally hard whether they succeed or fail"*

## 🏢 YC Office Hours: What Actually Happens

### Individual Office Hours
**The Key Question**: *"What's holding you back from moving faster?"*

- Not updates, not strategy questions
- Crystallizes priorities — there are only so many things that matter at this stage
- Founders often don't know what's slowing them down → the conversation uncovers it
- Probing questions lead to discovery of the real bottleneck

### Group Office Hours
**The Key Questions**:
- What are your goals for the next two weeks?
- What were your goals for the last two weeks?
- Did you hit them? If not, what got in the way?

**Two Core Purposes:**

**1. Combat Founder Loneliness**
- Starting a company is incredibly lonely
- Can't lean on employees ("I'm feeling really shitty today")
- Investors aren't really available
- Other founders are in the same situation — they understand
- *"When you ask a founder 'how are things going?' it's so emotional for them — it's never going well"*
- Hearing other founders' problems normalizes the experience
- Seeing how problems get solved is motivating

**2. Accountability**
- Founders are competitive — they don't want to look bad
- Coming back with "nothing worked" is not acceptable
- Even negative progress (learning what doesn't work) counts
- Creates an intense, productive 3-4 month period

**The Magic of Group Office Hours:**
- Best done in person, no computers, just pen and paper
- Founders often want to recreate this experience after YC
- Many continue ad hoc group office hours for years after the program

## ❌ Most Common Startup Mistakes

### #1: Not Talking to Customers
> *"If you don't talk to customers or users, you don't actually know what's important. And if you don't know what's important, it doesn't matter what you build."*

- This is where most failure stems from
- Early YC is largely about pushing founders to have customer conversations
- Key questions YC asks: What did you learn? What other software are they using? What are they paying for? How intense is the problem?

### #2: Being Afraid to Talk to Customers
- Technical people think software is the solution to everything
- Fear of rejection is the real barrier
- The math problem: 90% of people are NOT early adopters
  - They're incentivized to NOT take risks
  - They just want to continue what they're doing
  - Only 10% are early adopters
  - You have to reach 10 to find 1
  - Then you have to convince that 1 to get on a call
- *"This is common knowledge for salespeople but founders who've never done sales get surprised by the percentages"*

### #3: Not Being Technical
- Not knowing what it takes to build a successful technology company
- Not having technical founders
- Not being able to build the first prototype yourself
- YC screens heavily for this — doesn't accept many teams who can't build their first prototype

### The Root Cause of All Failure
> *"Make things people want. That's still true and it's always going to be true."*

## 🧠 Overcoming Fear of Customer Rejection

### The "Signed Up and Forgot" Insight
- Think about services you signed up for and never used
- Your reaction wasn't "I hate this" — it was indifference
- *"The worst thing that can happen to a startup is not that people hate what you're doing — it's that they're completely indifferent"*
- But indifference always gives you a second chance
- People have busy lives — they'll try again if you improve

### The Reframe
- Rejection should be put in context of the early adopter idea
- Most people don't care — they're not early adopters
- The narrower your solution, the fewer people will immediately want it
- But that's where you have to start — you can't build everything at once

### How to Actually Discover Pain
- Don't ask "do you have a problem with X?" — people don't know how bad their problems are
- **Watch them do it** — screen share, walk through their daily workflow
- Example: Non-technical people doing the same Excel task a million times by tabbing — they don't know it's painful
- *"You just have to watch those people to feel the pain — you can't actually ask them"*
- EV charging example: Just rent an EV and try all the non-Tesla chargers — you'll immediately feel how bad it is

## 🔧 The Technical Co-Founder Question

### Why Technical Founders Matter
- So many decisions you'll make are technical
- So many iterations rely on engineering
- You can't make the right decisions without understanding the technical side
- *"It's not like you have an idea of a product, you build a product, you're done — there are infinite iterations"*

### The Wrong Way to Think About It
- "I have an idea for a song, I just need a musician to help me make it" — this doesn't value engineering
- Giving a technical co-founder 10% equity signals you don't value what they do

### How to Find a Technical Co-Founder
1. Ask the best technical people you know — many have been waiting for someone to ask
2. Use YC's co-founder matching tool
3. Remove your fear of rejection and just ask

### When Non-Technical Founders Can Succeed
- If they deeply value engineering
- If they can build a team that feels like a founding team (even without co-founder equity)
- If they give equity to great engineers who become de facto founders
- The Calendly example: Ukrainian dev team built the first product AND drove initial growth

### Learning to Code Yourself
- Many successful founders learned to code themselves
- You don't need to be the best engineer
- You need to be sufficiently good to understand the value of engineering
- *"It's not that much more difficult than that"*

## 🏆 Attributes of Successful Founders

### The Core Four

**1. Determined to Win**
- Don't give up when things are hard
- Internal motivation that's infectious to people around them
- This is how they build great teams — people want to work for them

**2. Technical**
- More technical founders are more likely to succeed
- Not necessarily the best engineers, but technical enough

**3. Talk to Users and Move Fast**
- Don't wait for permission from investors or YC
- Continuous progress every day or week
- Doing it for customers, not for investors

**4. Excellent Communication Skills**
- Ability to communicate complicated ideas clearly
- Enjoy the communication part (correlates with enjoying fundraising)
- Storytelling motivates people around you
- *"It's rare that I've seen founders succeed where the founder isn't in some way an inspirational person or a good communicator"*

### The Outlier Factor
- To be a truly outlier company, something else has to happen that can't be put on a list
- Many investors said no to Airbnb because it was an illogical idea
- The ideas that end up succeeding often don't make sense to people
- *"We're not very good at knowing what's going to succeed — what we're good at is knowing what failure looks like"*

## 📊 The Founder Spectrum: Gustav's Takes

### Speed vs. Quality
- Not a spectrum — you need both
- Moving fast by talking to customers leads to quality because you know the problem
- Quality = what the customer thinks is valuable, not your personal definition

### Confidence vs. Humility
- Not a spectrum — you need both for different purposes
- **Confidence**: Critical for motivating people, attracting investors, proving doubters wrong
- **Humility**: Critical for inspiring people to want to spend time with you
- *"You need an unnatural amount of confidence to prove them wrong"*

### Execution vs. Strategy
- **Clear answer: Execution wins**
- Strategy is a business school concept for corporate environments
- Strategy assumes you can do multiple things at once — small startups cannot
- *"There's only one thing to work on and you can't really have a strategy session about the other things"*
- Getting to product-market fit IS your strategy — you don't have another one
- Good founders are execution-oriented with one clear priority at all times

### Autocratic vs. Collaborative
- Depends on stage — hard to generalize
- For small teams: you can't be purely autocratic
- What matters most: have a process for making decisions and stick to it
- Everyone needs to feel good about the process, even if not every decision
- Agree on how you decide together, then follow the process

### Product vs. Distribution
- Caring about product = caring about what customers care about (NOT your personal vision)
- Early stage: talk to users, do things that don't scale
- Later stage: you MUST figure out scalable distribution
- *"You can't start a startup with a growth team mindset — you have to unscale your thinking"*
- Growth mindset becomes valuable once something is working (1000 signups/day → how do you get to 2000?)

## 🌍 Climate Tech: The Full Picture

### Why Gustav Got Involved
- Saw the opportunity early — was ahead of the curve
- Helped write YC's climate-focused Request for Startups
- Has now funded 130+ climate tech companies
- *"I feel lucky to work with so many of these great founders — they are uniquely interesting people"*

### The Economic Case (Not Just the Mission)
> *"The world has decided we're going to stop doing the things that emit carbon. This decision has been made. The question is how it's going to happen."*

- Trillions of dollars moving from carbon-emitting to carbon-neutral activities
- Software transition is small compared to this
- Tesla alone: $600-700B market cap, only a couple percent of US new car sales
- *"We've only seen the beginning of this"*

### Why Now (The Two Big Shifts)

**1. The IRA (Inflation Reduction Act)**
- Massive US climate bill — onshoring supply chain, incentivizing the green transition
- Addresses carbon removal, home energy, heating, and more
- Europe now competing with their own incentives (afraid of losing battery factories to US)

**2. Corporations Are Now Customers**
- 3-4 years ago: "Talk to this person on this floor" — not empowered to decide
- Now: "We promised shareholders to reduce emissions by 2% per year — where do we start?"
- Showing up as paying customers, not just interested parties
- Fear of being the "Toyota to Tesla" — the last one to change

### The Decarbonization Framework
Two parallel tracks:
1. **Decarbonize everything** — transportation, energy, homes, shipping, aviation
2. **Carbon removal** — even if we do everything else right, probably not enough

### Interesting Portfolio Companies

| Company | What They Do |
|---|---|
| **C-Bound** | Carbon capture removal for ships |
| **Fleet Zero** | Electric ships |
| **Unravel Carbon** | Carbon accounting software (Singapore/Asia) |
| **Carbon Chain** | Supply chain and shipping carbon accounting (UK) |
| **Enode** | "Plaid for EV chargers and home energy systems" |
| **Statiq** | "Airbnb for EV charging in India" — fastest growing EV charging network in India |
| **Heart Aerospace** | Battery electric commercial airplanes |
| **Right Electric** | Electric aviation |
| **Pachama** | Forest carbon (Diego and Tomas — no domain expertise, just willingness to solve the problem) |

### Do You Need Domain Expertise?
- For software companies: **No** — solid PM background is more valuable
- *"Being a competitive PM coming from a really good culture of product management, knowing what good looks like — that's invaluable"*
- For founders: Can succeed either way — with or without domain expertise
- Pachama example: Founders had no forest expertise, just willingness to solve the problem

### The Electrification Pattern
> *"Whenever electrification is being applied, it turns out to be a more efficient way of doing whatever thing you were previously doing with combustion."*

- Almost no maintenance
- Cheaper to run
- Cleaner
- The limitation is always batteries (range)

## 🔮 YC's Ability to Predict Success

### The Honest Answer
- *"We're not very good at knowing what's going to succeed"*
- Cannot figure out who's going to be the really successful company in a batch

### What YC IS Good At
- Knowing what failure looks like
- *"If you fail, please do it in some new exciting way — not one we've seen 100 times"*
- Making companies not fail by being direct about how they might fail

### The Best Predictor of Success
- Each new office hour brings really exciting new stuff
- Not talking about the same thing from two weeks ago — they've already done that
- Continuous progress on a weekly or bi-weekly time scale
- *"If you're making continuous progress and that continues, that's a really strong correlation to some success"*

### Things That Are NOT Correlated with Outlier Success
- Being in a hot industry
- Being written up on TechCrunch
- Investors talking to you
- *"You'd be surprised how many of the things I just mentioned are uncorrelated to outlier success"*

- Won't buy rounds at bars — everyone pays for their own (don't want to be indebted)

- The dinner story: Waited in a friend's room while they had dinner — completely normal in Sweden
