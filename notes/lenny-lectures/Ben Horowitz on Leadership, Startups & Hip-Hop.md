## ✈️ The Pilot Story: Success Is a Series of Small Decisions

### The JFK Jr. Crash Lesson
- Ben asked a pilot what really happened in the JFK Jr. crash
- The press always tells the best narrative, not the truth
- The pilot's answer: *"All plane crashes are a series of bad decisions, and none of the decisions by themselves is that bad, but when you add them up, it's bad"*

### The 17 Bad Decisions
1. Getting to the destination was the priority (in flying, that can NEVER be the priority)
2. Wrong timing estimate for sunset (thought he'd be flying in sunlight)
3. When the plane was going down, trying to make it go up (he was upside down)
4. ...and 14 more compounding decisions

### The Core Lesson
> *"One decision leads to another. If psychologically you can take the sunk cost, then that gets you out of a lot of bad paths."*

- Success is a bunch of little things — not one big genius move
- A small thing that's hard to do doesn't seem to have high impact, but it leads to the next small hard thing
- *"If somebody were to write a story about me, they would be like, 'Then Ben did this really smart thing.' But it really wasn't like that."*

### The Shaka Senghor Connection
- Shaka was in solitary for 7 years — most people come out insane
- He came out massively self-improved
- His conclusion: *"I was in prison for 19 years. I was in solitary for seven. I come out. I can't rent an apartment. I can't vote. None of that was anything compared to what I did to myself."*
- The key insight: what people say about you and do to you only destroys you if you believe it
- *"If you go, 'That's not me,' you can overcome almost anything"*

## 🏃 Running Toward Fear: The Most Important Leadership Skill

### Why Hesitation Is the Worst Thing a Leader Can Do
> *"The worst thing that you do as a leader is you hesitate on the next decision."*

- The thing that causes hesitation: **both decisions are horrible**
- Business school teaches case studies where one option is clearly right — that's not reality
- Reality: rearchitect the product (miss features, miss the quarter, trouble raising money) vs. don't rearchitect (product can't get where it needs to go) — both are terrible

### The IPO from Hell Story
- Loudcloud went public with **$2 million in trailing 12-month revenue at 18 months old**
- Wall Street Journal wrote about how stupid it was
- BusinessWeek called it "The IPO from Hell"
- But the alternative was going bankrupt — which is worse
- *"If you look at March 2001, just look at the number of CEOs that hesitated on that and didn't do it and went bankrupt. It's a lot."*
- Ben knew the bad press stories would get written — and did it anyway

### The Psychological Muscle Required
> *"The psychological muscle you have to build to be a great leader is to be able to look in the abyss and go, 'Okay, that way is slightly better. We're going to go that way.'"*

- Should I fire the head of sales? → Don't want the conversation → Don't want to replace them → Don't want the bad PR → So you avoid it
- But not doing it is probably much worse
- *"That's why you have to run towards pain and darkness"*

### Can You Coach This?
- Ben's honest answer: not really
- *"I can point it out so that you recognize you were slow. But it's kind of like football — if you don't trust your eyes and run to the ball when you see it, you'll never be as good"*
- What Ben CAN help with: getting CEOs over the fear of the conversation itself
- Example: CEO with an asshole CTO — Ben helped reframe the conversation from "you're an asshole" to "you're not effective as a CTO because you can't work with other parts of the organization"

### The Value of Leadership
> *"If everybody agrees with the decision, then you didn't add any value because they would have done that without you. The only value you ever add is when you make a decision that most people don't like."*

## 💪 Being Liked vs. Being Respected

### The Anthropological Reality
- Humans are wired to want to be liked — it's survival instinct (so they don't throw you to the lion)
- This causes people to tell others what they want to hear

### The Board/Investor Role
- As a board member: you MUST tell CEOs what they don't want to hear
- *"That's the most important thing you're going to say. And yes, they're not going to like it when you say it. But over time, it could save the company."*
- All the most important things Ben has said to CEOs are things they did not want to hear

### The Jensen Huang Insight
- CEOs who had an easy run early find it very hard to develop the "run toward fear" muscle
- CEOs who gutted it out for decades (like Jensen at Nvidia) develop it naturally
- *"You kind of have to get to near death to get yourself to do that"*

## 🏢 Who Should (and Shouldn't) Start a Company

### The John Reed Quote
- John Reed (CEO of Citigroup) told Ben: *"The only reason to start a company is because you have an irrational desire to do so, because it's not worth the money."*
- He didn't even quantify how much money — just said it's not worth it
- Ben's reaction after selling Loudcloud for $1.6B: *"Wow, that wasn't worth the money."*

### The Right Motivation
- NOT: "I want to make a lot of money" — terrible reason, won't get through the hard times
- YES: An irrational desire to do something larger than yourself, to improve the world in some way
- *"That is your purpose, and if you don't feel that, you'll never get through it"*

### Two Valid Paths to Starting
1. **Product forces you to build a company**: You build something, the world wants it, you need a company to deliver it
2. **Abstract mission first**: HP model — "we have to build technology for the world" → then figure out what the engineer next to you needs ("next bench" philosophy)

### The Better Alternative
> *"I think you're way better off taking a job offer at Meta and just doing that. That's a way better deal."*

## 📊 Managerial Leverage: The CEO vs. VP Distinction

### The Databricks Story
- Ali Ghodsi was VP of Engineering, Ben made him CEO
- In first 1-on-1, Ali was trying to coach and develop low performers
- Ben's advice: *"You don't make people great. You find people that make you great, that make the company great, that you learn from, not the other way around."*

### Why This Is Different for CEOs
- VP of Engineering: you CAN develop people — teach them to be better engineers and managers
- CEO: What do you know about being CFO? VP of HR? Marketing?
- *"The idea that you're going to take somebody who isn't world class at marketing and make them world class when you don't know anything about marketing is a dumb idea"*

### What Managerial Leverage Actually Means
> *"If I have the ideas about what your department should do next, if I'm pushing you to move your organization forward — that's no leverage. Leverage is if you're telling me what you should do and how you can push the company forward."*

- When you feel like you're not getting leverage — when you have to say "why aren't we doing this?" — that's when you need to make a change
- Ali is exceptional at this: he really cares about people but does not hesitate when he's losing leverage

## 🔄 When to Replace a Founder CEO

### The Consistent Pattern
1. Founder becomes CEO without knowing how to run a big organization
2. Makes expensive mistakes (down rounds, lost customers, screwed up products)
3. Loses confidence
4. Starts hesitating on decisions
5. Senior people get nervous and jump into the void
6. Political dysfunction emerges
7. Company becomes dysfunctional

### The C-Minus Student Advantage
> *"The median on the CEO kind of test is like 18. It's not like 90."*

- Better to be used to failing — straight-A students are disconcerted by constant D-minuses
- A D-minus is fine as long as you don't get the F (run out of cash, lose the whole thing)
- *"Keep going, you know"*

### How a16z Helps with Confidence
1. **Network access**: 600 people at the firm building your network — you can call any CEO or executive on day one
2. **CEO-to-CEO conversations**: Talk to Ben and others as a peer, not as investor-to-CEO
3. **The CEO Barbecue**: Ben would barbecue for 500 CEOs in his backyard — Larry Page and Zuckerberg in the same space as you
   - *"Wow, like I must be important. I'm here with all these guys and we're just hanging out having a drink, eating barbecue."*
   - When you go back to your company, you feel like you ARE somebody

## 💡 The Most Counterintuitive Startup Lessons

### Lesson 1: Don't Hire Senior Executives Too Fast
- Common advice: hire senior executives as soon as you get PMF, let them scale it
- Ben's view: build the team slowly and deliberately, paced to your ability to integrate and manage them
- If you bring in a bunch of senior people before you understand how they map to your company → you start deferring → they build empires → politics → dysfunction

### Lesson 2: Founder Mode ≠ Never Hire Experienced People
- Some people have taken "founder mode" to mean never hire anyone with experience
- That's also bad advice
- Example: Arcelon at Databricks (PhD in computer science) was running sales
- Ben told Ali: *"You're going to have to hire somebody who knows sales. There's a litany of stuff — customer knowledge, territory splitting, rep profiles — that you can only learn by doing."*
- *"The idea that companies go, 'We're just not going to hire that because we're in founder mode' — that's also a mistake"*

### The "Don't Be CEO at Home" Misunderstanding
- Ben told a CEO: "Don't be CEO at home"
- Another VC interpreted this as: "You've got to unplug sometime"
- Ben's actual meaning: *"You can't go home and boss your family around"*
- *"These little snippets of advice that VCs give because they watched some podcast are all stupid. There's a lot of depth to these things."*

## 📝 Good Product Manager, Bad Product Manager: The Legacy

### Why Ben Wrote It
- Had a very talented PM team at Netscape who weren't getting the core concept
- Team included: David Weiden (now at Coastal Ventures), Ragu Raguram (later CEO of VMware)
- *"I was getting so mad. I can't yell at people anymore. I have to explain to myself."*
- Written in frustration — the aggressive tone ("bad product manager" = "bad dog") reflects the emotion

### What It Was Really About
> *"The job is fundamentally a leadership job, and it's a tricky leadership job because nobody is actually reporting to you."*

- PMs were getting wrapped around the axle on tasks: pitching customers, writing PRDs, press relations
- None of those are the job
- The job: get a product into market that customers love, that's better than anything else in the world
- *"It doesn't matter if you write a good spec or you have a good interview or you do this or do that. What matters is that the product wins."*

### What Still Holds Up
- The mindset: be the leader, get the thing done
- Leadership is undervalued and underestimated
- *"Leadership is the most powerful thing"*

### What's Outdated
- The task-specific details were written for Ben's group at Netscape in 1996
- The specific tactical advice doesn't apply to modern product management

### The PM as Mini-CEO
> *"There's always this kind of sense that the PM is not the mini CEO. How dare you call yourself that? I actually think that's exactly what the PM is."*

- People get jealous over stewardship — that's the challenge of management in general
- A CEO isn't the one who has every idea and gives every order
- A CEO consolidates good ideas, prioritizes them, decides which to do, gets everyone on the same page with high fidelity
- That IS a CEO function — and it's what a PM does within their product

### The Creative Writing Insight
> *"The ideas that you have, the things that you write in five minutes end up being much better than things you write in five weeks."*

- The thing you've already synthesized so much that you just have to write it out — that's the best stuff
- Musicians, writers, everyone has this same experience

## 🤖 AI: Bubble or Not?

### On Sam Altman's "We're in a Bubble" Comment
- Sam is a CEO; Ben is an investor — different incentives for what to say
- *"If it's a bubble, the one thing you should invest in is him and not all these guys chasing after him"*
- Also: the press loves when you say investors and entrepreneurs are idiots
- *"Whether he believes that or not, that was a super smart thing to say"*

### Ben's Actual View: Not a Bubble
- *"Anytime everybody thinks it's a bubble, it's not a bubble — you need capitulation for a real bubble"*
- The dot-com bubble comparison: unit economics didn't work, businesses didn't work
- AI businesses ARE working: revenue growth unlike anything seen before
- ChatGPT works amazingly — *"We've never seen that before. Not even Google."*
- Companies going from $0 to $800M in a year

### What Could Go Wrong
- Technology is very immature despite how well it works
- Competitive positions may not be sustainable
- New number one could emerge, prices could drop
- But that's different from a financial bubble

### Where the Opportunities Are

**1. Infrastructure Layer**
- Who can run open source models cheapest with lowest latency?
- *"That's going to be extremely valuable — whoever has that"*
- Google historically very good at this; OpenAI building Stargate

**2. Foundation Model Layer**
- Very selective — need to be able to raise $2B+ without much product progress just to be competitive
- Less than 10 people in the world who can do this (Ilya, Mistral, Fei-Fei Li)

**3. Application Layer**
- *"The thin wrapper around GPT" narrative is wrong*
- Same mistake was made in the 80s: "thin wrapper around an RDBMS" — that's what Salesforce was called
- Cursor example: built 14 different models to understand how a real developer works — that's not a thin layer
- *"There are many many applications like that"*
- Applications are more complex AND stickier than people thought

### The Enterprise AI Opportunity
- Enterprise data is different from internet data
- Access control is hard with AI (who has access to what information?)
- Semantic issues: 10 enterprises have 10 different definitions of "customer"
- Databricks having success here because of these complexities

### The Waymo Lesson
- What took so long wasn't snow or heavy rain
- It was people: the human driving 75 in a 25 zone
- *"The number of rare but important crazy things that humans do is very high"*
- This applies to all of AI — the fat tail of human behavior is enormous

## 🌍 Why America Must Win at AI

### The Core Argument
- The right amount of decentralized power is essential for human flourishing
- Concentrated power (communism, fascism) always leads to catastrophe — it's a systems problem, not a person problem
- *"It doesn't matter if it was Mao or Pol Pot or Ceaușescu or Stalin — everybody died because when you give anybody that much power, nobody has a chance"*

### The US System's Advantage
- "We hold these truths to be self-evident" — the rules are God's rules, not the president's rules
- You're under the law, not under the person
- This distributes power in a way no other system has achieved as well

### The Historical Pattern
- Countries that industrialized first and best got economic, military, and cultural power
- Countries that were slow to industrialize fell into dangerous systems (Russia, China)
- *"Looking forward, that's going to happen again — but it's going to be AI"*

### What Ben Hears Traveling the World
- *"We need you to succeed. Don't destroy the dollar. Don't fall behind AI. Don't overregulate it too early."*
- Everyone is counting on the US to win this

## 🏈 The Adam Newman/WeWork Investment Defense

### Ben's Principle
> *"You don't judge a person by the worst thing that ever happened to them."*

- Learned partly from Shaka Senghor
- Most bad things people do don't get a miniseries made about them
- Judge people on what they do WELL — that's where you see the talent

### What Adam Did Right
- WeWork: *"Name a more important commercial real estate brand than WeWork — you can't"*
- What went wrong: combination of inexperience and nobody around him who would tell him the truth
- *"To throw away a guy on that — the world was so mad at us for not throwing him away — is just a big mistake"*

### The Investment Philosophy
> *"We're investing in strength, not lack of weakness. I want to know: are they world class? Do they have a world class strength that can beat anybody?"*

- Everybody's flawed — help them deal with the flaws
- Surround them with people who can handle that
- Put the right person on the board who can talk to them
- Ben goes to ALL of Adam's board meetings because he's good at "killing a guy who's that confident" when needed

## 🏗️ Shaka Senghor's Culture-Building Lessons

### Building Trust from Zero
- The fundamental challenge: bringing in people from broken culture who have no trust
- Like a military operation — without trust, you're completely dysfunctional
- *"How do you build trust from zero is a very interesting problem. And he's a genius at that."*

### Tactic 1: Eat Together
- Made everybody eat lunch together in the gang
- Simple but powerful for building rapport, relationship, trust
- *"People really underestimate how powerful just that idea can be"* — especially in remote work

### Tactic 2: Morality Applied Internally AND Externally
- You had to be good to your word both inside and outside the gang
- Normally in a gang (kinbased culture): loyalty only to the in-group
- Shaka's rule: you can't be devious outside the gang either
- *"Is it okay to lie internally? Probably not. Is it okay to lie to a customer? In some organizations it is. But in Shaka's organization, that's as big a cut as lying internally."*
