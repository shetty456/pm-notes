*Lenny's Podcast*

- Meteoric rise pattern: VP in 3 years at first job, COO in 2 years at Nuna, CTO in 3 years at Netflix

## 📈 The Economics Advantage in Tech

### Why Economists Are Valuable in Tech
> *"Economics is a flavor of data science. It is a set of technical skills and a way of framing certain problems or solving challenges."*

- Economics was undervalued in tech before the data science boom — harder to make the argument then
- Now: economics is clearly complementary to other forms of data science
- Useful for problems you might not immediately think to bring data to

### The Specific Skill: Incentives and Unintended Consequences
> *"Understanding incentives and thinking about unintended consequences — that's true both in internal leadership and externally."*

**Internal applications:**
- How do we clarify priorities?
- How do we motivate a company?
- What are the unintended consequences of a management decision?

**External applications:**
- How do we think about what Netflix is to consumers?
- How do we think about competition?
- What might rational people do that we didn't expect given certain incentives?

**The key framing:**
- Rational economics: shouldn't intelligent people behave this way?
- Behavioral economics: given certain incentives, what might you see that wasn't optimal or expected?
- Combines elements of psychology with planning ahead — thinking through cause and effect

## 🚀 The Secret Sauce for Rapid Career Growth

### Elizabeth's Pattern
- Associate → VP in 3 years (Analysis Group)
- Manager of Data Science → COO in 2 years (Nuna)
- VP → CTO in 3 years (Netflix)

### The Key Ingredients

**1. Dedication (Not Long Hours)**
> *"The dedication piece really isn't about long working hours. It's more about how much I care about excellence."*

- Holding herself to a very high standard
- Being responsive when someone sends something
- Following through on commitments in the timeline promised
- Being on time to meetings
- *"Other people are relying on me and I want to show up for them"*
- The motivation is the team, not personal ambition

**2. Being Part of a Team and Setting Others Up for Success**
- Building partnerships where you genuinely care about other people's success
- Learning from others, helping others learn from you
- Getting better outcomes for the business together

**3. Technical-to-Non-Technical Translation**
> *"A big part of my role is: can I explain how we're going to approach technical problems in a way that builds competence with the content team? Can I understand their content strategy in a way that sets the technical teams up for success?"*

- This skill was trained at Analysis Group: quantitative work communicated to judges and juries for economic cases
- Became a relative advantage as she moved into more senior roles
- The Netflix live content example (WWE): requires close partnership between content org and product/tech org — translation is essential

**4. Observation and Learning from Others**
> *"I'm a relatively introverted only child, so I observe a lot, which means I learned from other people."*

- Watching what other people are doing
- Thinking about: "Could I learn something from them? Is this something I want to be able to do myself, or does it not fit my style?"
- Learning by osmosis and observation
- Being surrounded by amazing people and leveraging that

## 🎯 Maintaining a High Bar: The Three-Part Framework

### Setting Expectations
- Be explicit about what excellence looks like
- Don't assume expectations are clear — share them
- Example: telling the team before a quarterly business review that she'd rather they spend time thinking about what conversation they want to have, not polishing the document for 20 more hours

### Giving Specific Feedback
- Be direct about when something isn't meeting the standard
- Be specific about what would it take to get to the bar
- Do it in private, not on stage in front of everyone
- *"I don't do it live in the big meeting in front of all the people where the presentation doesn't go very well. I do it afterwards where it feels like a safer space."*

### Helping Fill the Gap
> *"Jumping into the document and helping. Let's work on this together."*

- Don't just give feedback — help them get there
- This makes people feel they're in a safe space
- The manager wants them to be successful, not to encourage failure
- *"Through that, help people uplevel themselves so the next time around they know the expectations and they've had help getting there."*

### The "Last 5%" Principle
- Elizabeth's mother: *"The last 5% is the 5% that really matters."*
- The extra effort you put into something to make it world-class
- Consistent with company cultures where that's the general expectation — you don't feel like you're doing it alone

### Excellence ≠ More Hours
- A perfectly polished document for a QBR is NOT the goal
- The goal is: did we get to the outcome we wanted because we were thoughtful about it?
- *"I would much rather have someone spend the time thinking about what's the conversation we really want to have and how do I tee that up, not could I spend another 20 hours to make it look like everything's perfect."*

## 🏆 Netflix Culture: High Talent Density

### Why It's the Foundation
> *"We can't really have any of the other aspects of the culture, including candor, learning, seeking excellence and improvement, freedom and responsibility if you don't start with high talent density."*

- High talent density is the means to the end, not the end itself
- Rooted in Reed Hastings' founding belief that there could be a different approach to building a company
- People derive fulfillment from the excellence and outcomes, not just from being there

### What High Talent Density Requires

**Uncomfortable candor:**
- Giving feedback when expectations aren't being met
- Being direct about what could be better
- Receiving that type of feedback yourself
- *"It's an uncomfortable amount of candor and feedback in order to keep that bar high."*

**Timely decisions about fit:**
- Making a call in pretty timely fashion if someone's not able to meet the bar
- Either: this isn't the right role, or Netflix isn't the right place
- This doesn't come naturally to humans — hence the need for the Keeper Test

### The Keeper Test
> *"If this person on my team came to me and said, 'I'm leaving today. I have a different opportunity and I would like to take it,' would I do everything I could to keep them at Netflix?"*

- If not → have the tough conversation about whether they should really be there
- If you might be a little relieved if they left → that's the signal
- The goal: people shouldn't be surprised by this conversation
- Only possible if you've been giving feedback along the way
- In ideal state: a mutual observation
- In practice: not always that smooth

**How it's operationalized:**
- It's a mental model, not a formal process
- Managers are expected to ask themselves this with some frequency
- People ask Elizabeth directly: "Am I passing your keeper test?"
- Becomes part of regular 1:1s — a way of saying "am I meeting your expectations?"

### No Performance Reviews
> *"We don't have a practice that a lot of other companies do where we would think about reflecting on a rating of how things are going."*

- Annual 360 feedback cycle: request and receive feedback from many people — but it's just for the value of the feedback, not an input to an output
- Annual compensation cycle: performance is reflected in promotions and compensation
- The ideal: timely, direct, real-time feedback — not waiting for the annual cycle
- The challenge: easy to rely on the annual 360 as a crutch
- *"I do get about 300 pieces of feedback. Some of those things are on things that happened six months ago and I think, 'Oh, I wish you had told me this at the time.'"*

### Addressing the Stress/Hunger Games Concern
> *"In my personal experience, I have felt a lot more at ease by having these conversations than by not having them."*

- Not knowing how you're doing creates MORE stress than having the conversation
- The high bar can feed a sense of "am I doing well enough compared to everyone else?"
- But knowing — even if the feedback is hard — is better than not knowing
- *"My manager says I'm doing a great job, or my manager says I'm not doing a great job but gave me concrete things I could do better."*

### Hiring for Talent Density
> *"We're looking for the new perspective or the person who's actually going to make us stronger as a team."*

- Not: does this person have skill A, skill B, skill C? Check — they'll fit in this box
- Instead: are they going to help us identify the right problems to solve or new ways to solve existing problems?
- Looking for additive skills, additive perspectives, people who push thinking
- *"We're looking for the things that make people exceptional or even stronger than the team we've got."*
- Personal top of market compensation: highly competitive pay, but not meant to be golden handcuffs
- The best people want to work with the best people — maintaining the bar keeps the best there

## 🔓 Freedom and Responsibility

### What It Looks Like
- Not prescriptive about how people solve problems
- Not a lot of process around the work
- Room for contribution across all levels of the team
- People can make large innovations to engineering systems, introduce new ways to think about metrics and experimentation

### Why It Requires High Talent Density
> *"The lack of process and prescriptiveness is all hinging on we've got amazing people who are smart, but even better have strong judgment."*

- Without high talent density, freedom and responsibility would be "very hard if not dangerous"
- You have to have amazing people if you're not going to have strict guardrails

### What Came Out of This Freedom
- Innovations in content delivery network
- Innovations in encoding
- Innovations in discovery and personalization
- *"A lot of the stuff that Netflix has succeeded in came from creating space for people on the team."*
- Not driven by a leader saying "I think this is a priority" — driven by individual contributors with great ideas

### The Current Challenge
> *"The trick is finding the sweet spot so that we can operate efficiently at this type of scale without snuffing out some of what was the core beauty of the culture."*

## 📢 Radical Candor at Netflix

### Transparency as Candor
- Elizabeth takes notes in leadership meetings and shares them with the whole organization
- *"Context, not control — part of my job is to make sure that people have the context they need to do their jobs well."*
- Shares things that "probably push the boundary a little" in terms of what the team knows about what's happening

### The IC Levels Story (A Candor Example)
- Until two years ago: no individual contributor levels at Netflix — all engineers were just "senior engineers"
- Introduced IC levels two years ago — a "change rollercoaster"
- Most companies would: "We got past that change, we're living it, don't reflect on it"
- Elizabeth's approach: did a postmortem/retro on how it's gone
- *"This was a big change for us. It hasn't all gone perfectly. There's a lot that we can do better in how we implement levels at Netflix."*
- *"I would rather share that information than pretend it's all gone swimmingly."*
- This level of candor and reflection helps build community and trust

## 📊 The Data and Insights Team Structure

### What Makes It Unusual
- At Netflix's scale, most companies embed data teams in business lines OR separate them functionally (data engineers vs. data scientists vs. analytics engineers vs. consumer researchers)
- Netflix has resisted this: **centralized team that is both functionally diverse AND works on nearly every area of the business**

### The Benefits

**Functional excellence:**
- Are we really world's best data engineers, world's best data scientists?
- Continuing to improve from a functional and technical perspective

**Better career paths:**
- More mobility across teams
- Feels like a team with functional expertise and lots of different problems to solve

**Cross-pollination of ideas:**
- More exposure to different problem spaces

**Objectivity — the most important benefit:**
> *"Our job is not to tell the story that someone wants to hear with the data or to solve the problem that someone thinks is most important. It's for us to have our own perspective about things."*

- Truth tellers who can be curious in a way that might not fit with an embedded structure
- Uplevels the whole organization

### Consumer Insights + Data Science = Superpower
- Consumer Insights team: content screenings, UX research, accessibility research, global/regional consumer understanding
- Combined with data science and engineering: can tackle problems like recommendations using both attitudinal research (qualitative and quantitative) AND behavioral research
- *"We talk about it as a superpower internally in combining those skill sets."*
- Avoids the classic tension: "Data's telling me this, user research is telling me this — what do we do?"

## 🤝 Staying Close to the Ground

### The Practices
**Biweekly office hours:**
- People sign up for 20-minute slots
- Feels like speed dating but creates connection
- People book them out many months ahead

**Ask Me Anything sessions:**
- Teams of different sizes
- Anything is fair game — get to know her as a person, hear questions, be candid about what she can and can't answer

**Leadership meeting notes:**
- Takes notes in leadership meetings
- Shares them with the whole organization
- Creates a flow of communication between her and the team

**The key insight:**
> *"It just wouldn't happen if I didn't make it a priority. And through those types of sessions, I become more approachable so people know: you can send me a Slack message, you can send me an email, and I'm going to respond to you as quickly as I can."*

## 🧘 Being Present and Building Community

### Why Elizabeth Is Present in 1:1s
> *"I'm genuinely curious about how someone's doing, how I can help them, what they're excited about — that's authentic."*

- Preserves a lot of time for 1:1 conversations
- Treats them as "pretty sacred"
- The human connection is part of what she enjoys about work

### The Long-Term Payoff
> *"Some of my greatest friends and connections, including people like Ali, are people I met along the way professionally. You get back what you give."*

- Advice to others: *"This is a small community. Think about what you're investing in other people because that will matter down the line for yourself too."*

### The Morning Practice
- Early mornings are protected quiet time
- Daily check-in: how are things going? Why am I feeling anxious? Why am I feeling excited?
- Not a formal meditation or journaling practice — more of a muscle built over time
- Inspired by Jeff Bezos's "puttering" approach: no meetings until 10am, just reading and thinking
