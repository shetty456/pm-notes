## 🔭 Skill 1: Creating a Compelling Vision

### Why Vision Is Everything
> *"The reality of the product development cycle is that it's so messy, it's so chaotic. In order to ensure that moments that are not discouraging but rather learning opportunities for your team, it's so important to be anchored on that singular vision."*

- Any step along the way feels like forward progress when anchored to a clear vision
- The question to anchor on: **"If you and your team do your job correctly, what does the world look like?"**

### How to Craft a Compelling Vision

**1. Cross-pollination of functions**
- Cannot go into a vacuum and come out with a compelling vision
- Must be fundamentally inseparable from your users AND your team
- Research insights → feel what users feel
- Designs and prototypes → communicate what the future world looks like
- Engineering → root it in feasibility
- Referenced: *The Medici Effect* — when people from different places come together, that confluence leads to innovation

**2. Communicate it visually, not just verbally**
> *"Words will only get you so far."*

- Figma is a fundamentally detail-oriented culture that practices what it preaches about visual communication
- Vision pitch format: **pain point → solution → proof point → pain point → solution → proof point**
- A testimonial from a user on top of a prototype is more compelling than words alone

**3. Create a single unified artifact**
- Common mistake: research readout → design crit → product review (separate deliverables)
- Better: one combined deliverable where every team member feels incredible ownership and passion
- At Figma: decks are made in Figma, with prototypes built even before a project gets a green light

### The FigJam Vision Example
- Insight: brainstorms are the most common meeting in FigJam files
- Why brainstorms work: they're democratizing — ideas can come from anywhere, not just the loudest person
- Vision: **"How can we create a world where the generative nature of a brainstorm is the norm in other kinds of meetings?"**
- Features that followed: music, voting, Kudos Boards, templates for democratic meetings
- The vision gave meaning to every feature decision

### Getting Prototypes Without Design/Engineering Skills
- AI is dramatically lowering the floor (Cognition/Devin, Replit as "technical co-founder")
- Use hackathons: walk around asking people to work on your idea with you
- *"Never letting your own skills stop you from going out there making a pitch and then turning that into reality"*

## 💪 Skill 2: Building Strong Conviction Quickly

### The Foundation: Product Sense = Intuition
- Meta distilled PM into two capabilities: product sense and execution
- Product sense = good intuition = built through insatiable curiosity and constant user conversations

### How to Build Intuition
> *"Once you start having enough conversations, you build this almost repository or library of conversations that you can draw from as you're making product decisions."*

- Talk to users at every chance — even at dinners, with friends in tech
- Each conversation adds to the library; patterns emerge over time

### The "A- Idea" Framework (from Yuhki)
> *"When you go into research, you want to go in with something that's at least an A- idea. Because if you talk to users and you learn something about it, that's awesome — get to an A+. If not, at least you're not at a B."*

- Putting out an idea, even if wrong, is a much better catalyst than starting from nothing
- People are much more likely to react to an idea than to nothing
- If it's right → "Oh my God, yes!" If it's wrong → they take you in a different direction

### Strong Opinions, Weakly Held
- Be willing to kill your darlings if external signal tells you otherwise
- Need agility to pivot when you get contradicting information

### Communicating Conviction Without Steamrolling
- Be explicit about confidence levels: "I feel medium confidence on this" vs. "I feel really strongly about this"
- Use phrases: "This is my hypothesis" / "I do not have an opinion here, I defer to you"
- Create a culture where everyone feels comfortable speaking up
- *"Strongly minded PMs can go into a conversation and be like, 'I think we should do X,' even though they feel medium confidence. And the rest of the room says nothing because they think you're so certain."*

## 🗣️ Skill 3: Direct Communication

### The Two-Way Street Requirement
> *"Directness only works if it's two-way. If it is one person being really direct with another person and then the other person being afraid to talk, you will end up in probably a not great relationship."*

- Meta's phrase: **"Feedback as a gift"** — deeply believed
- Feedback should be constantly flowing, not just at official cycles

### How to Create a Culture of Direct Communication
1. **Ask for feedback first**: "Hey, do you have feedback for me?" — this makes the other person feel heard and creates reciprocity
2. **Act on feedback immediately**: As soon as possible, put it into effect and communicate what you're doing about it
3. **This incentivizes the other person to do the same**

### Setting Expectations When Joining a Team
- Always let new teammates know: "I am very direct and if you disagree with me, I want to know that"
- This prevents the situation where people stay silent because they think you're too certain

## 🔥 Skill 4: Building Hype

### Internal Hype vs. External Hype
- **Internal**: Getting buy-in and everyone inside the company fist-pounding for a product to get built
- **External**: Getting users stoked about milestone occasions and launches

### The Key Insight
> *"You can't really create hype for something you don't believe in. The only way to create hype is to get people to see what you see."*

### Tactics for Internal Hype

**Leverage large company forums:**
- Sales Kickoff (SKO): insisted on including a demo of the new product even when it was barely built
- Maker Week (hackathon): used it to pitch the new product to the whole company
- Config (annual showcase): major external hype moment

**The January momentum problem:**
- Over winter break, everyone forgets what happened last year
- Solution: get your product visibility in the first company-wide forum of the year

**Push your product beyond what its current stage merits:**
> *"If you push your product to get visibility, maybe even beyond what the current stage of product development merits, you have really incredible learnings."*

### The FigJam Birthday Party Example
- One-year FigJam anniversary → didn't do an anniversary party, threw a **birthday party**
- Easter-egged new features throughout the product hidden under birthday presents
- Sent users on an Easter egg hunt throughout the day
- *"Not to advance the company's goals, but to make me feel special, to make me feel happy"*

### Product-Specific Hype
- Hype for FigJam = cheeky, fun, personality-driven
- Hype for Figma = niche design capability that unlocks a long-standing pain point
- Hype for Apple = world's best unboxing experience
- *"Leaning into a product's brand in order to figure out what is the optimal way to generate hype with your audience"*

## 👂 Skill 5: User Immersion

### The Core Practice
- Ask users about your product at every opportunity — dinners, friends in tech, anyone
- Ask **non-users** why they're NOT using your product — often the most insightful conversations
- Non-user conversations reveal: marketing perceptions, barriers to adoption, latent needs

### The FigJam Non-User Example
- Asked people from her previous team why they weren't using FigJam
- Led to investing in features making it easier for non-designers to get started on the canvas
- Launched a placeholder experience showing use cases and previews on the canvas

### For Different Company Types

**Consumer products (Meta):** Everyone has an opinion — ask anyone
**Niche B2B products:** Immerse yourself in the circles where your users are
**Infra/security products:** Build tight relationship with sales team; hop on sales calls

### Operationalizing User Insights

**The FigJam/Sales cadence:**
- Set up recurring cadence with sales team
- Share what's coming → get their input on prioritization
- Their input → roadmap signal; your signal → use cases they can push with customers
- Made a Loom video walking through her weekly FigJam usage → sales team distributed it to companies

**Storing insights:**
- Slack integration with Asana emoji reaction
- Any feedback from sales or company → automatically becomes a task in backlog
- Weekly grooming of that backlog

## 🎭 Skill 6: Culture Building

### Why Culture Matters for PMs
> *"Roadmaps change, products change, but feeling a connection to the folks that you're working with makes you much more durable as a team. When times get tough, your gut instinct is to rally together rather than to jump ship."*

- Passion breaks down into two things: passion for the vision AND passion for the people you work with

### The Hot Seat Game
- Started at first PM offsite post-pandemic (March 2022, sub-15 person PM team)
- Each person gets 2 minutes on the clock; everyone else can ask them anything
- Can decline to answer; kept friendly and comfortable
- Foundation: personality tests (Strengths Finder) earlier that day created context
- Result: spread throughout the entire company — PMs played with their teams, execs played with exec team
- Now a tradition: anyone who joins the team gets put in the hot seat

### The Figgies
- Oscar-style awards ceremony for the PM team
- Transformed the Figma boardroom (called "Bigma") with red carpet, gold curtains, Oscar trophies with names
- Absurd categories: "most likely to name their child Figma," "most likely to go their career without writing a PRD"
- Everyone had to give an acceptance speech
- Total cost: very low — everything ordered on Amazon, assembled in an hour
- *"Don't be intimidated by any idea of being too large to take on. Just go for it."*

### The Culture Principle
- Figma core value: **Play** — work should be fun, gathering should be fun
- Culture is often set top-down, but innovation comes bottoms up
- Having a value like "play" creates space for these things to be celebrated

## 🔄 Skill 7: Dealing with Change

### The Design Nation Origin Story
- Founded a national design conference for students while at Princeton
- Original plan: build within an existing Princeton organization (funding, resources, connections all set)
- That organization was skeptical → had to build from scratch with nothing
- Lesson: *"Having the ability to, in the absence of formal backing, still chase after something and maybe pivot the way that you're thinking about it"*

### The .edu Email Hack
- Cold emailed executives with a Princeton .edu email address
- Designers like Daniel Burka and others would hop on calls: "Oh my God, this was such a problem when I was in college"
- Grew into a multi-year conference bringing together students from around the world

### The Adaptability Principle
- Be very adaptable when it comes to resourcing
- Suddenly, fundraising might be more important than speakers
- Building a hype landing page might be more important than the actual content
- *"Just being quite adaptable when it comes to resourcing is very important"*

## 🚀 Zero-to-One at Large Companies

### Why Companies Need to Stay Entrepreneurial
> *"If you are not constantly thinking about what's next, defining the industry standard, seeing around the corner from your competitors, you will get taken over."*

### Figma's Hackathon Culture
- Maker Week: entire company goes on pause for a week
- Most monumental launches came from hackathons:
  - Multi-edit (launched recently) — multi-year initiative
  - Jambot (AI plugin in FigJam) — came from AI hackathon
  - Widgets platform — originally a hackathon project
- *"Most people think hackathons are only a time for engineers to build. That's one of the biggest mistakes ever."*

### The Three Things Needed to Bring an Idea Into Existence

**1. Have the right idea (empathy)**
- Constant user conversations about pain points AND general perceptions AND other tools they use
- Understand their full end-to-end tooling usage
- Ensure it ladders into a company goal

**2. Secure buy-in (vision)**
- Rally leadership and team behind the idea
- Optimism that borders on delusion: *"You need to have insane, almost like reality distortion field where you don't hear the word no, or at the very least, you translate it into a not yet"*

**3. Make it spread like wildfire (hype)**
- Get it to a point where someone joins the company and asks, "What is that flame burning there?"

### The Keeper of the Flame Metaphor
> *"In Greek mythology, Hestia is the keeper of the hearth, and it is her job to always keep the hearth burning, even when all the gods peace out to go on their various quests."*

- The person pushing a zero-to-one idea is the Hestia — keeper of the hearth
- Job: stoke the flames when they're at risk of dying out AND ensure the idea can spread like wildfire

### The Hackathon Pitch Strategy
- First few pitches of the new product were not successful
- Third time stuck: Maker Week hackathon
- Was simultaneously hosting the hackathon AND pitching her own idea
- Key scrappiness move: swapped out the FigJam icon in the file browser to the new product icon (2-line code change)
- *"Taking what exists and changing the minor things in order to communicate what is different about your idea versus what exists today"*
- Demo day at end of week → company-wide forum → colleagues evangelizing the idea alongside you

### The Staging/Dogfooding Strategy
- Figma has a multi-month+ staging/dogfooding process
- Putting something on staging early does two things:
  1. Gets feedback to mature the product
  2. Makes people invested in the product — *"I shaped that part of the product"*
- *"Product development is a team sport. It takes everyone to make something successful."*

## 🪞 What Mihika Thinks She's Not Good At

### The Double-Edged Sword of Conviction
- Downside: without a history of working together, people may wonder if you're pushing something because you believe in it vs. because users actually need it
- Solution: constantly highlight user proof points

### Scrappiness
- Edits product review decks minutes before presenting
- Stays up until 3 AM the night before
- Other people don't always love this — "Maybe let's start earlier next time"

### Getting Consumed by Details
- Sometimes you want to defer decisions, not get into every detail
- Important skill to learn: knowing when to let go

### The Key Principle
> *"As you scale your team, it's really important to be self-aware of what those blind spots are and to hire for that. Because you want individuals to be spiky and you want the team to be well-rounded."*

## 💡 Understanding Motivations: The Key to Running a Team

> *"Understanding motivations is, in my opinion, one of the keys to running a team successfully and driving an idea forward."*

- Different people want to be involved in product visioning to different extents
- Some engineers want a solution handed to them (the exciting part is finding the technical solution in code)
- Others find it hard to feel passionate about something they haven't contributed to
- **Ask directly**: "How much do you like being involved in product decisions?"
- Take decisions in the open when possible — give everyone the opportunity to voice their perspective
- *"Cater to the individual, as opposed to the average"*

---

## 🔑 The Scope Mindset

> *"My take is that your scope is the world. Nothing should ever be perceived as being out of bounds."*

- Don't think of the project currently in flight as your scope
- Take a step back, understand the company vision, find what you're passionate about within that
- Anyone can found something — inside an existing company or from scratch
- Inside a company: distribution advantage, technical/platform advantage, less risk
- Outside: more executive control, faster movement, different team composition
