## 🚫 10 Growth Tactics That Never Work

### Why This List Exists
> *"Growth is a fairly new field. There's a lot of misinformation out there in terms of what growth actually is and how it should be done."*

- Growth teams have a higher head of growth firing rate than even CMOs
- People come in with expectations, growth doesn't happen, the axe comes, the cycle repeats
- Social media creates a flood of out-of-context tips that don't apply as patterns

---

### ❌ Tactic 1: Hiring a Growth Team Too Soon

**The mistake:**
> *"There is a huge misconception in the field that in order to get growth going, you need a growth team. Absolutely not true."*

- Founders and founding team must figure out how to grow to first $1M, $5M, $10M ARR
- Some companies don't create growth teams until $100-200M ARR
- A shiny resume cannot wave a magic wand and produce viral campaigns and signups

**Two prerequisites before hiring a growth team:**
1. **Solid PMF**: You have a solution to a problem, customers are coming in AND retaining
2. **Data**: If you have 10 users, that's a Google Sheet, not data. You need volume to run analysis, create hypotheses, and apply experimentation

**The counterintuitive advice:**
> *"The longer you wait, the better it is, because that way your entire company will be trained to be responsible for growth as opposed to putting this one island with a growth team."*

**Sales vs. Growth — when to hire which:**
- Sales-led companies: hire sales way before growth (sales does your monetization, activation, retention)
- Product-led companies (self-serve revenue): growth hire should come BEFORE sales
- If product acquires via SEO/SEM AND sales closes: both matter, but growth comes in sooner

---

### ❌ Tactic 2: Hiring a Growth Team to Fix a Declining Business

**The mistake:**
> *"If you have the overall business slowing down, your head of growth is destined to fail."*

- Growth team can maybe lift things 10-15% — and that's the upper end
- The reason business is slowing down is much deeper than not having a growth team
- Growth can amplify great PMF; it cannot fix a broken one

**What growth actually does:**
- Amplifies great product-market fit
- Helps you grow faster once you're already growing
- Cannot fix go-to-market strategy problems or core product strategy problems

**When to hire growth at an established company:**
- First, stop the decline — plateau it
- See visible trajectory that core product and marketing teams can reverse the degradation
- Ideally, see some signs of life and pockets of potential
- THEN put growth in to blow it up

---

### ❌ Tactic 3: Redesigning Your Homepage/Marketing Site to Drive Growth

**The mistake:**
> *"Never ever once have I seen a rebrand or redesign, especially of a marketing site, produce good performance results."*

**The pattern:**
- New CMO comes in → redesigns website to reflect personal taste → promises acquisition will go up → never materializes

**The reality:**
- A redesign is always a step back in performance
- Best case scenario: net neutral results + better ability to optimize toward something bigger
- Typical cost: $1M+ in agency fees, 8-10 months of development, lots of arguments about shades of blue

**If you must do it:**
- Don't promise performance lift at launch
- Model out and forecast for a hit
- Give yourself 3-6 months minimum to optimize back to good standing
- Bake in that post-launch optimization work — most teams forget to do this and just move on

**The product redesign parallel:**
- Same applies to product redesigns — you're creating a new starting point that's lower than your current optimized experience

---

### ❌ Tactic 4: Blindly Copying the Competition

**The mistake:**
> *"Copying competition is like the fastest way to mediocrity because you'll never be a leader if you copied somebody else."*

**Why it fails:**
- Every experience is unique to their customer and their channel
- You don't even know if you're seeing their actual experience or a personalized/test variant
- Fails 95% of the time when applied directly

**What Elena actually does:**
- Tears down companies constantly (thousands of Gmail accounts for sign-ups)
- Uses competition for **inspiration and ideation starting points**, never as a destination
- Looks for common elements across many products → those are likely winning elements
- Uses web archives to see how pages looked historically and extrapolate results
- Has extensive Miro boards with everyone's onboarding flows, pricing pages, sharing models

**The right approach:**
> *"I use it as an input into ideation, but I never use it as a destination for the result."*

- Never skip: ideation, design, user research, customer interviews, experimentation
- Use competition to avoid the "empty start problem" — but always do authentic implementation

**The benchmarks warning:**
- Benchmarks are dangerous because definitions vary wildly company to company
- Even "signups" is defined differently everywhere (all traffic vs. new traffic vs. new IP address)
- Use benchmarks as directional input, not as targets to blindly set

---

### ❌ Tactic 5: Thinking Your Problem Is Unique

**The mistake:**
> *"I am 99% sure your problem has been felt by somebody somewhere in probably many, many places."*

- Re-engineering a solution from scratch is time lost to market with huge opportunity cost
- People are afraid to admit they don't know how to solve a problem → they start from scratch

**The right approach:**
1. Look at competition for how they solve the problem
2. Find people who have solved this problem and just ask them (LinkedIn, X, your network)
3. Patternize your solutions — fit problems into existing patterns or frameworks

**Elena's Miro community example:**
- Tasked with standing up community, had never done it before
- Talked to Caroline from Atlassian who had done it
- Caroline immediately asked: "Are you talking about a user community, agency community, or partner community?"
- Mind blown — hadn't even considered all the angles
- Advice: start with agency/partner communities for faster results
- Saved 6-8 months of failure cycles

> *"You need to really put your ego aside and get some help faster than trying to hit every single failure point along the way."*

### ❌ Tactic 6: Growth Team Owning and Prioritizing Paid/Organic Channels Over Earned Channels

**The mistake:**
> *"When you're doing organic search or paid search, you're making Google richer. An algorithm can giveth, but algorithm can also taketh away at any point."*

**The core insight:**
- Paid channels (SEM, social ads) = you're paying for someone else's distribution
- SEO = you're building in Google's channel, not yours
- Algorithm-dependent channels can disappear overnight

**What an earned channel is:**
- Product-led growth acquisition: virality, word of mouth, user-generated content
- Nobody else can compete with you in it — your competitors cannot buy those eyeballs
- Referrals from people to people are everything

**The Dropbox example:**
- Over 50% of acquisition comes through sharing
- When you share a file, the recipient becomes aware of Dropbox (brand awareness)
- The act of sharing also activates the recipient (they see how the product works)
- A percentage of recipients sign up → stable earned channel nobody can compete with
- Dropbox had its own growth pod focused entirely on optimizing sender AND recipient experience

**Why this matters now:**
> *"Search as an interface is changing towards AI interface and the AI interface is giving a lot less credit to all of the content."*

- Content is becoming a database with a new UI (AI) being built on top
- Less control over this new UI than you had over Google
- Cost of acquisition in paid channels will only go up

**The advice:**
- Diversify — don't have all acquisition reliant on someone else's distribution
- Invest in earned channels: virality, user-generated content, referrals, community
- This is applicable to more products than people realize

### ❌ Tactic 7: Not Overlaying Multiple Growth Models Over Time

**The mistake:**
> *"Not overlaying every single way that you can grow through product, through marketing, and through sales as an evolution is a huge mistake."*

**The S-curve reality:**
- Most growth loops spin out their ability to produce meaningful results within 5-7 years
- Dropbox's sharing loop at 17 years is an anomaly
- Law of Shitty Clickthroughs (Andrew Chen): over-optimizing the same thing has minimal returns

**The three growth models to layer:**
1. Product-led growth
2. Marketing-led growth
3. Sales-led growth

**The timeline:**
- Every 18 months: introduce something new
- Every 5 years: something big has to start taking a big portion of volume
- Don't wait until slowdown — growth loops take 6 months to 1.5 years to start producing visible revenue

**The Miro Verse example:**
- User-generated content library of Miro boards
- Took 18 months before putting metric expectations on it
- Eventually became both an engagement engine AND an acquisition engine

**The 20-25% rule:**
- Allocate 20-25% of growth team's time annually to introducing new growth loops or channels
- Don't goal it immediately on monetization or acquisition — you'll cut it at its knees
- Give it room to evolve

### ❌ Tactic 8: Not Hiring Advisors

**The mistake:**
> *"Any growth team that does not have an advisor is a growth team that is underperforming."*

- Even Elena hires advisors for herself every time she takes an operator role
- You can get access to anybody for one hour a week — you just have to pay them
- Creates a network of people with data points and patterns you can tap into

**How to vet advisors:**
> *"Do some sort of workshop with them on the problem that you're experiencing. See them in action."*

- Don't hire until you do a paid workshop on your actual problem
- Any advisor cagey about what they know is not the right advisor
- After the workshop, hire on ongoing retainer
- Evaluate every single month: did they add value?
- Some advisors only need 3 months; some stick for 4-5 years

### ❌ Tactic 9: Testing Everything (Experimentation Paralysis)

**The mistake:**
> *"If every single one of your initiatives that you're doing on growth is an experiment, that's a problem. It's almost like a disease, like a paralyzing disease."*

- Slows down progress, velocity, and learnings
- Creates terrible consequences for growth team output
- Teams become afraid to do any change without testing first

**The heuristics:**
- If you cannot collect the sample size in a month, don't test it — just do pre vs. post
- Pre vs. post is powerful: assess impact, still roll back if needed
- Statistical significance (95%) is just a directional data point, not gospel

**When to actually run experiments:**
- Really big strategic pivots that need validation
- Very high traffic real estate where 0.1% difference = millions of dollars

**The right approach:**
- Trust intuition more — data is only good if you have enough of it
- Release → 24-hour readout → 7-day readout → 28-day readout → come back a year later
- Failure is going to happen regardless; the question is how fast you can fail and learn

### ❌ Tactic 10: The Fire Round (Quick Ones)

**10a: Color Optimizations**
> *"A blue is a blue is a blue. As long as it's accessible and bright enough, off you go."*
- Don't test shades of blue vs. green — early 2000s tactic, doesn't work anymore

**10b: Third-Party Signups (Adding Auth Options)**
- Exception: developer products should have GitHub auth
- Productivity products: email is fine, Gmail is nice but won't drive more acquisition
- Not going to create incrementality, improve activation, or improve retention
- It's a customer experience investment, not a growth tactic

**10c: One Email Wonders**
> *"One email will never do anything. Too few people open it, too few people click it."*
- 25% open rate at best, 40-50% if you're lucky
- Never test a change in one email — you need the whole strategy
- Email is a series, a communication strategy, not a one-off

**10d: Removing Friction as a Goal**
> *"If you ever have a line item on your roadmap that says 'simplified onboarding,' please cross it out."*
- Simplifying is an action, not a problem
- The actual problems: people are confused, people don't know where to go, people aren't educated enough
- Simplifying might be a solution to those problems, but it can never be the problem itself
- Removing steps or simplifying to oblivion loses product identity
- Fix cognitive load friction, not just step count

## 🔧 3 Favorite Growth Frameworks

### Framework 1: Growth Loops
> *"Anybody who thinks about growth in the funnel fashion versus understanding what the growth loops are is missing out on the ability to create a sustainable growth engine."*

- Action → reaction → generates another action → self-contained flywheel
- Resources: Reforge, Brian Balfour, Casey Winters, Andrew Chen

### Framework 2: The Race Car Framework (Lenny + Dan)
- Separates growth initiatives into:
  - **Engines/loops**: keep spinning continuously
  - **Fuel**: paid marketing dollars you add
  - **Turbo boosts**: big user conferences, major launches
  - **Optimizations**: pouring oil into the engine so it performs correctly
- Helps teams understand which initiatives are long-term vs. short-term and their expected results

### Framework 3: Adjacent User Theory (Bangaly Kaba)
- Think about users outside your ICP, outside your core user
- Growth team can bring adjacent users in and add oomph to your product
- Doesn't require expanding product-market fit — just optimizing their experiences
- Adds additional growth without product changes

## 💼 Contrarian Corner: Full-Time Jobs Are Not the Best Way to Monetize Your Skills

> *"Full-time jobs are not the best way to monetize the skill that you have. It's one of the packages that everybody should evaluate and take advantage of, but too many people blindly default to that package."*

### The Problem with Defaulting to Full-Time
- Boxes you into one company that may not fit culturally, skill-wise, or ambition-wise
- People set titles as goals → get there → find themselves dissatisfied or depressed
- *"People managing is a terrible job if you don't enjoy it and you don't know if you're going to like it until you actually do it"*

### Other Options to Explore
- Freelancing/contracting
- Advising and consulting (Elena's primary model)
- Interim engagements (predetermined end date)
- Fractional engagements (part-time)
- Courses, newsletters, content monetization

### Career Optionality as the North Star
> *"My goal professionally is actually to have options so I can choose what I want to do — what fits my life right now, what fits my skills and personality, and what makes me happy."*

**The reframe:**
- Instead of: "What will get me to the next title?"
- Ask: "What can I do next year that will increase my option pool?"

**Evaluating opportunities through this lens:**
- Does staying at this company for one more year increase my options?
- Keep it the same?
- Or potentially decrease them?

**The important caveat:**
> *"You have to earn your right to unlock optionality and earning that right does usually lie within full-time jobs."*

- Full-time roles are wonderful for building expertise and learning
- It's not a one-way door — you can go full-time, then advisory, then back to full-time
