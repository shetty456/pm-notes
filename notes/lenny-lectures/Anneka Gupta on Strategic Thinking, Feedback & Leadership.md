## 🎯 Skill #1: Having Fun — The Abundant Mindset

### The Core Insight
> *"When I was able to switch my mindset and say, 'I'm actually going to figure out a way to have fun with this,' it actually changed my entire approach to dealing with super difficult situations."*

### The Origin Story
- Had to change out all her direct reports in very short order
- Initially felt scared and daunted by the amount of change ahead
- Someone gave her advice to reframe: find a way to have fun with it
- This reframe opened her mind to more opportunities

### How to Actually Do It

**1. Ask: What can I learn from this situation?**
- What positive outcome can I get even though it's incredibly challenging?
- Look for the growth opportunity embedded in the difficulty

**2. Bring humor into meetings**
- Start on a light note
- Elevates your own mood AND adds levity for others
- *"As a leader, it's not just about your own mindset, but how are you transferring that mindset to the people that you're working with"*

**3. Manage energy, not just time**
- Know your worst time of day (hers is 5-6 PM) and don't schedule hard meetings then
- Don't skip lunch — it decreases energy
- Schedule difficult work (strategy decks, hard meetings) during peak energy hours
- *"Being able to manage my energy levels and figuring out how to schedule my time for my energy has really allowed me to have that abundant mindset in all situations"*

## 🏗️ Founder Mode: Two Perspectives

### Working WITH a Founder in Founder Mode (as CPO)

**The positive framing:**
> *"Having a founder that can effectively operate in founder mode means that I can go have a conversation with the CEO and say, 'Hey, look, we have this huge opportunity and these are the things that aren't working, and I need your help to help figure out how we can move the needle more substantially.'"*

- The founder is a **lever** — use their power to get important things done
- Activate the CEO as an ally for initiatives you believe in

**When the founder pushes an idea you disagree with:**
1. First, ask yourself: *why* are they pushing this? What's the underlying objective?
2. If the mechanism is wrong but the objective is right → have a conversation about alternative approaches
3. *"Maybe instead of looking at option A here, we should have explored these three other options instead"*
4. If it's a pet project with no strategic merit → decide: is this the hill to die on?
5. Make the judgment call: what will make or break the company vs. what can you let go?

### Operating IN Founder Mode (as a Product Leader)

**The core practice:**
> *"Understanding the details of the business and asking questions and understanding to the utmost extent you can — what's working, what's not, what are the financial goals, are we on track — getting into that level of depth is super important."*

- Collect information even when you don't immediately act on it
- Having the information lets you decide *where* to make course corrections
- You can choose to do nothing with information — but you want to have it

**How to course-correct without stomping on the team:**

**Tactic 1: Ask them to present their strategy first**
- Have them come in and present their best thinking
- Ask questions and make suggestions in that forum
- *"I'm not coming in and rewriting the entire strategy, but I'm giving them an opportunity to present their best thinking and then trying to figure out how do I take that and make that better"*

**Tactic 2: Share a hypothesis, not a directive**
- *"I was talking to security leaders in our customer advisory board recently and I heard this piece of feedback and this is what it made me think about our strategy — what do you think about that?"*
- Opens discussion without shutting it down
- Avoids the senior person problem: "I'm going to come in and say something, and because I'm one of the more senior people in the room, no one is going to say if they have a concern"

**Tactic 3: Pick a strategic set of areas**
- Focus on: what will kill the business if we don't get right? What are the biggest opportunities?
- This gives clarity to the team about what's most important
- Rally the troops around those specific things

## 🧠 What "Being Strategic" Actually Means

### The Two-Part Definition
> *"When people say 'I want someone that's strategic,' what they're really saying is: (1) I want someone that can come up with and articulate a compelling and simple why behind the decisions and the direction of the company and product. And (2) I want someone that's going to champion and be a change agent to do things that may be hard but actually best for the long-term interest of the product or company."*

**The critical insight:**
- Having ONE without the OTHER means people won't see you as strategic
- Great articulation of why + only small ideas = not strategic
- Championing big ideas + can't articulate the why simply = not strategic
- **You need both**

### How to Actually Get Better at Being Strategic

**Tactic 1: Summarization**
> *"Bringing people together, lots of different voices into a room and hearing what they have to say and at various times in the conversation summarizing what people are saying and summarizing what that means in terms of the direction that we could go in."*

- Even without adding a new idea, effective summarization is perceived as strategy
- It makes people feel heard
- Helps move circular discussions forward
- Creates a checkpoint: "Is this correct or do we need another turn?"

**How to do it in practice:**
- *"Hey, let me pause here for a second and try to capture what has been said. This is what I've heard: our customers are having these challenges, we feel this is the way to solve them, we have a right to win in this way, therefore we're going to take this action. Is everyone in agreement or is there some dissent?"*
- End with a question — invite discussion, don't just declare
- Can also do it in Zoom chat without interrupting the flow
- Can write on a whiteboard while people are talking

**Tactic 2: Make ideas one click better**
- Don't try to do something radically different — take existing ideas and make them slightly better
- Think from an outside-in perspective: customer problems + go-to-market, not technical implementation
- *"I'm seeing a material difference in terms of the number of big ideas that we're going and pursuing overall as a company and also the quality of the strategic thinking that I can bring to the table"*

## 📚 Decision-Making: The Historian Approach

### The Core Insight
> *"When I joined Rubrik, I came into an organization that had a lot of history that I just didn't know about. So one of the things I decided to do was to really understand what happened in the past."*

**What to study when joining a new company:**
- Products that were launched but weren't successful — and why
- History of how decisions were made and why
- Perceptions of different people in the organization
- What baggage people carry around certain ideas

**Why this matters:**
- Helps you make better decisions going forward
- Helps you understand the resistance you'll face: *"We've tried that before"*
- Lets you address that resistance with context: *"I know we tried X before, but here's what's different now"*

**Ongoing practice:**
- Even today, when she hears about old projects: *"Tell me about this project. What happened with it? How did we decide to do this?"*
- Being curious about history gives context for current decisions

### Making Decisions with Incomplete Information

> *"It's not about making the right decision, it's about making the decision."*

**Why this matters:**
- You're always operating off imprecise information as a product leader
- Once you commit to a decision, you learn more post-commitment
- You move out of the hypothetical into real, high-fidelity information
- *"As long as your decision is like 70% right, you can iterate on that 20-30% in either direction"*

**Creating a culture of risk-taking:**
- State the hypothesis clearly upfront: "This is what we believe, this is the evidence we have, this is what we don't know"
- **Reward the learning, not the outcome**
- *"If we're constantly learning, it is okay if we make bad decisions, but we learn from them and we get better for next time"*
- Even bad decisions teach you something about customers or the business you can use elsewhere

## 🤝 Navigating Difficult Personalities

### The Core Mindset
> *"I try to embody the mindset of feeling like and believing that I can work with anyone."*

### The Two-Step Approach

**Step 1: Understand what drives them**
- What do they really care about? Company success? Personal career? How they're perceived?
- *"If I need something from them, what is it that I can do to motivate them to find what I need from them important?"*
- It's like building a product: they have a desire, how do you get them to care about what you care about?

**Step 2: Shift from frustration to gratitude**
> *"Instead of feeling anger or frustration with the person, trying to shift that to a mindset of feeling gratitude and a positive emotion about them — I look at them and I ask myself what can I learn?"*

- Everyone has something they can teach you: communication style, how they marshal people, visionary ideas
- Study them, be curious about them, learn from them
- When you actually learn something, you'll feel genuine gratitude

**How to figure out what someone wants:**
- Talk to people who have worked with them successfully before
- Talk to people who work for them (they've figured out what makes them tick)
- Talk to peers who have navigated them successfully

## 💬 Giving and Receiving Hard Feedback

### Receiving Hard Feedback

**The process:**
1. **Feel it** — let yourself experience the emotional reaction (upset, defensive, etc.)
2. **Don't react** — whether it's a few hours or a few days, don't respond immediately
3. **Come back curious** — ask: where is this feedback coming from? Why am I getting this?
4. **Investigate** — go back to the person, talk to peers with more context
5. **Decide** — is this valid? Does it require action?

> *"All feedback I think is valid. People's feelings are valid, but it doesn't mean that you need to do something about all of those things."*

**How to decide what to act on:**
- Anchor on: what is best for the company and what does the company need from me right now?
- Must-haves vs. nice-to-haves — sometimes there are too many must-haves to address nice-to-haves
- *"When you come back curious, people then want to give you more feedback because they know that you're listening"*

### Giving Hard Feedback

**The setup (most important):**
> *"I care so much about you and I'm giving you this feedback because I want you to be successful and I want you to be able to reach the pinnacle of what I know you can accomplish."*

- Say this explicitly — don't just hope they understand it
- Show it in your body language
- This makes the other person much more receptive

**The delivery:**
- **Be direct** — passive-aggressive feedback is the worst kind
- State clearly: this is what you are doing OR this is how you are being perceived
- Give three concrete things they can do to change
- Do significant prep before the conversation: how can I frame this so it doesn't feel like an attack?

**The framing trick:**
- Say "this is how you're being perceived" rather than "you are doing X"
- This gives them the benefit of the doubt: their intention may differ from their impact
- Opens a better conversation: "What can you do to change that perception?"

**The setup conversation:**
- Start by asking: "What do you want to do? Where do you want to go in your career?"
- This lets you tailor the feedback to what actually matters for their goals
- CPO-track feedback is very different from "I never want to manage people" feedback

**Be part of the solution:**
- Don't just dump feedback and say "go figure it out"
- Brainstorm with them
- Share personal examples of how you've navigated similar situations

## 🚀 Breaking Into Product Management

### The Easiest Path: Internal Transfer
> *"Doing it within the same company is a lot easier than trying to switch companies and switch jobs at the same time because when you're within a company, you've already built credibility."*

**The strategy:**
1. Crush your current job to build credibility
2. Raise your hand and interact with the product team
3. Take on projects that give you PM experience
4. Build a relationship with a product leader who might take a chance on you

**What you bring from other functions:**
- **Customer support**: deep knowledge of what problems customers are calling about
- **Sales**: how to sell the product, what resonates, objection handling
- **Engineering**: how the product is built, technical nuances

### What to Say in Interviews
- At a 20-person startup: worth saying "I want to become a PM" — they may not have a product team
- At a 5,000-person company: probably don't say it in the interview for a different role
- Once inside: find ways to make relationships with product leaders and take on PM-adjacent projects

### What New PMs Most Misunderstand
> *"Students would say, 'Can you teach me how to use Figma? Can you teach me the tools?' What you need to learn to be successful is how to take very ambiguous situations and consistently drive more and more clarity over time."*

- The mismatch: people think they need tools and process
- What they actually need: the mindset and skills to clarify ambiguity
- Tools are learnable; the mindset is the hard part

### Most Common Paths Into PM (from Stanford class)
1. Joining product-adjacent roles and transitioning in
2. Going to small startups as first PM or doing "PM plus plus" (chief of staff, etc.)
3. Very few go directly to established companies as PMs without prior PM experience

## 🧘 Building the Positive Mindset: Journaling

> *"The mindset that you bring to your work is actually the most important thing over anything else that you can do."*

### The Practice
- Journaled every day from age 13 to 23
- Still does it today when she has a lot of thoughts, especially negative ones
- Write down all the thoughts going through your head
- Explore: why am I feeling this way? Why am I getting triggered?

**Why it works:**
- Takes abstract rumination and makes it concrete
- Allows you to break it down and understand the why
- Some things you realize are irrational → let them go
- Other things you uncover: what do I need to do differently? What's within my control?
- *"It's like doing cognitive behavioral therapy on yourself"*
