## 🔑 What Is Jobs to Be Done?

### The Core Premise
> *"People hire products, they don't buy them. They hire them to make progress in their life."*

- The lie Bob was told as an engineer: "Build it and they will come"
- JTBD reframes the question from "how do I build this?" to "what progress are people trying to make?"
- Key insight: **a struggling moment causes demand** — not the product itself

### The Snickers vs. Milky Way Example
**Snickers job:**
- Missed last meal, lots of work ahead, running out of energy
- Want to get back to tasks as fast as possible
- Competes with: protein drinks, Red Bull, coffee, sandwiches
- It's essentially a meal replacement — masticates into a ball, absorbs stomach acid

**Milky Way job:**
- Eaten after an emotional experience (positive or negative)
- Usually eaten alone, taking time to regroup
- Competes with: a glass of wine, a brownie, a run
- The caramel melts immediately — it's consumed like a drink, not food

**The key insight:** JTBD reveals the **true competitive set from the demand side**, not the supply side. Snickers and Milky Way barely compete with each other despite being nearly identical products.

## ⚡ The Four Forces of Progress

Bob uses a visual framework with four forces that determine whether someone will switch to a new product:

**F1 — Push (context that makes them leave the old product)**
- Something in their current situation that's not working
- Without a push, they can't even see your product — we're creatures of habit

**F2 — Pull (attraction to a new outcome)**
- The desired new state or outcome
- What they're hoping to achieve

**F3 — Anxiety of the New**
- Every time you show someone something new, it creates anxiety
- More features can actually increase anxiety, not reduce it

**F4 — Habit of the Present**
- The inertia of what they're already doing
- The cost of leaving the familiar

### The Critical Equation
> *"If F1 and F2 are not greater than F3 and F4, they're not going to move."*

**The counterintuitive insight:** Adding more features increases pull (F2) but also increases anxiety (F3). Reducing friction (F3 and F4) can be more effective than adding features.

### The Condo/Moving Example
- Sold condos to downsizers (3,000 sq ft → 1,500 sq ft)
- People were canceling 6 weeks later because they didn't know how to get rid of their stuff
- Solution: **raised the price** of the condo, included moving + 2 years of storage
- Result: **30% increase in sales**
- Lesson: reducing the frictional coefficient was more powerful than improving the product

## 🔍 The Demand Side vs. Supply Side

### Supply Side Thinking (Wrong)
- Looking at the product and asking: what else can we do? Where can we be better?
- Competitive benchmarking against similar products
- Building roadmaps based on features

### Demand Side Thinking (Right)
- Starting with struggling moments that exist before any product
- Understanding what people will **fire** when they **hire** your product
- Roadmaps based on struggling moments, not features

> *"Struggling moments and opportunities all exist before there's a product."*

### The Southern New Hampshire University Example
- 2010: Found 50-60 anomalies — people paying full price but not coming to class, watching everything online
- These people were older, had tried college before, had responsibilities
- The struggling moment: "I need to go back to school but can't do it the traditional way"
- Result: SNHU now has 200,000+ students — one of the largest universities in the world
- **The product didn't create the demand — the struggling moment already existed**

## 📊 The Six Phases of Buying

Bob identifies six phases customers go through (not just the moment of purchase):

1. **First Thought** — something triggers awareness of a problem
2. **Passive Looking** — problem aware, solution unaware; learning phase
3. **Active Looking** — both problem and solution aware; framing a solution
4. **Deciding** — making trade-offs between options
5. **First Use** — the initial experience with the product
6. **Ongoing Use** — building the new habit

### The Autobooks Example
- Company was running one demo for all prospects, then trying to close
- Problem: customers were at different phases of their buying journey
- Solution: broke the demo into three versions:
  1. **Story-based demo** (for passive lookers): background on the problem
  2. **Alternatives demo** (for active lookers): showing all the options
  3. **Choices demo** (for deciders): giving them ways to move forward
- Result: **Sales cycle cut in half, 4x conversion rate**

> *"We meet them where they are as opposed to where we want them to be."*

## 🎤 How to Interview Customers (The JTBD Method)

### What Makes JTBD Interviews Different
- NOT traditional market research (asking what they want)
- Based on **criminal and intelligence interrogation** that feels like therapy
- Focus on people who have **already made the switch** — not people who say they will

### The Three Layers of Information to Extract
1. **Functional energy**: time, space, effort, knowledge
2. **Emotional energy**: how they feel (frustrated, overlooked, inadequate)
3. **Social energy**: how they want others to perceive them

### Key Principles

**Only talk to people who already tried to make the progress:**
- Don't ask people what they want to do — ask them what they did
- For zero-to-one products: study people who used competitors, not your product

**Don't use a discussion guide:**
- Following a script means you miss the most meaningful information
- Use the framework (pushes, pulls, anxieties, habits) as a lens, not a script

**Get them to the "edge of language":**
- When they run out of words, bracket it: "Was it more about this or more about that?"
- You know neither is right — but it forces them to elaborate

**Play things back incorrectly:**
- When you say something wrong, they'll correct you and talk more
- The moment someone says "yes," there's nothing more to say

**The layers of language:**
1. **Pablum layer**: "How was your day?" "Oh, it was good." (meaningless)
2. **Fantasy/nightmare layer**: exaggerated positive or negative
3. **What actually happened**: the real story you need to get to

### How Many Interviews?
- From a causal mechanism perspective, patterns repeat around **7-8 interviews**
- Bob does **10, no more than 12**
- Better to do two rounds of 12 than one round of 24
- Use design experiments to sample efficiently without needing statistical significance

### The Critical Warning
> *"Bitchin' ain't switchin'."*

- Just because people complain about something doesn't mean they'll do anything about it
- Basecamp: everyone said they'd leave if there were no Gantt charts — nobody actually left
- Never trust what people say they're going to do; only trust what they've already done

## 🏗️ How to Apply JTBD (Practical Steps)

### For Established Products
1. Find 10 people who recently bought your product
2. Don't ask about the product — ask about **why** they bought it
3. Questions to ask: What was going on? What were they hoping for? What were they worried about? What did they have to give up? How did they convince someone else?
4. Just listen to the story first

### For Churned Customers
- Talk to people who left — they're still making progress, just somewhere else
- Their story reveals the struggling moment that caused them to leave
- Nobody wants to change, so understanding why they changed is gold

### For Zero-to-One Products
- Find what people will **fire** when they hire your product
- Go interview users of the competitors they'll be leaving
- Example: For a new marketplace, study eBay, Etsy, and Craigslist users

### Clustering vs. Segmenting
- Don't look for themes across all interviews
- **Cluster** them — find the pathways (sets of pushes, pulls, anxieties, habits that work together)
- Most products are hired to do 3-5 different jobs that are in conflict with each other

## 🏢 Case Studies

### Intercom
- Found people hired Intercom for four very different reasons
- Instead of building four products, they **turned off features** not relevant to each pathway
- Acquire pathway → competed with HubSpot
- Support pathway → competed with Zendesk
- Changed pricing model to match the competition for each job
- Positioned as the right next step between "nothing" and HubSpot/Zendesk
- Result: Valued at over $2 billion

### Basecamp
- Customers demanded Gantt charts and resource allocation
- JTBD revealed: the people who wanted those features weren't actually leaving without them
- Adding those features would have destroyed the simplicity that made people join in the first place
- Lesson: **Following your best users can destroy your core value proposition**

### The Dining Room Table
- Built condos for downsizers who said they didn't want a dining room table
- JTBD revealed: the dining table was the **emotional bank account of their entire life**
- People wouldn't move until they knew where the table was going
- Solution: Built a symbolic space for the dining table (too small to actually use)
- Result: **22% increase in sales** by doing the opposite of what customers said they wanted

## ❌ When JTBD Doesn't Work

1. **When there's no real choice** — health insurance given by employer (vs. car insurance you choose)
2. **When the habit is so deep they can't remember** — chewing gum (people can't remember buying it, but can remember chewing it)
3. **When companies want to confirm their existing hypothesis** — JTBD is hypothesis-building research, not hypothesis-testing research

> *"If I try to help you build the case to make the jobs what you think they are, it doesn't work."*

**Alternative for these cases:** Ethnography, studying frictional points in the system, prototyping alternatives, studying the "little hire" (how they use it, not why they bought it)

## 🔄 The Three Flavors of JTBD

### Bob Moesta's Approach
- Very qualitative, organic
- Combination of process, practice, and skills
- Starts from the demand side — only people have jobs, not products
- Best for startups and new-to-world products
- Hypothesis-building research

### Clay Christensen's Approach
- More theoretical and philosophical
- Strategic thinking framework
- Derived from the same dataset as Bob's approach
- More about the thinking, less about the method

### Tony Ulwick's Approach (Outcome-Driven Innovation)
- Comes from the supply side — what can our product do?
- Very systematic, hundreds of steps, very prescriptive
- Valuable for large organizations with lots of risk, regulation, complex systems
- Requires a very disciplined organization to follow

## 💡 Key Insights and Principles

### On Struggling Moments
> *"Struggling moments is the key. They are freaking everywhere in our lives and there's only certain contexts when all of a sudden we realize we have to do something about it."*

### On Trade-offs
> *"Choose what to suck at and figure out the trade-offs that you need to make and make sure that your trade-offs map the trade-offs of the customer."*

- Nine times out of 10, products fail because they made a trade-off the customer didn't agree with

### On Value
- Value is not just the outcome — it's also where you start
- If someone starts very low and ends up high, they value it more than someone who starts slightly lower and ends up at the same place
- Overshooting can actually make customers want a price discount

### On Innovation
> *"Innovation happens when people change."*

- Most people study the momentum of where people are
- JTBD studies what causes people to **change their direction**

### On Roadmaps
- Features-based roadmaps are obsolete within 90-120 days
- Struggling moments-based roadmaps are durable because the job exists before and after any technology
- *"Nobody saw ChatGPT coming. But if I talk about the struggling moments I'm trying to go after, I realize there are multiple ways to solve it."*

### On Irrational Behavior
> *"The context makes the irrational rational. The moment you hear a story and you go, 'I can't believe that,' nine times out of 10 it's because you don't have the rest of the story."*
