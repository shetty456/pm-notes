## 🤔 Why Strategy Is So Hard

### Three Reasons

**1. Intellectually Challenging: It's Integrative**
> *"It answers a bunch of questions that have to fit together and reinforce one another."*

- Not like "what do you want for lunch?" — more like "what diet will keep you healthy?"
- Requires breakfast, lunch, dinner, snacks to all fit together
- Multiple interdependent choices, not a single decision

**2. Emotionally Intimidating**
- Strategy involves making choices to do some things and NOT other things
- Requires cutting things off and being held accountable
- People are afraid to make decisions they'll be judged on

**3. Poor Training**
- Business schools have fallen in love with the **Resource-Based View (RBV) of the firm** — which Roger considers useless
- Strategy consulting firms do almost no strategy anymore (post-merger integration, digital transformation dominate)
- *"People aren't prepared educationally for it, and if they're not prepared educationally, they sure don't get prepared for it in companies"*

---

## 🏫 The Academic Drama: Why Business Schools Teach Strategy Wrong

### The Resource-Based View Problem
- Born out of **jealousy of Michael Porter** — academics didn't like his prominence
- They caricatured Porter as "the positioning school" (which he never actually said)
- RBV says: build resources and they will come
- The fatal flaw: any resource useful somewhere isn't necessarily useful elsewhere
- Doesn't answer: which resources to invest in building, and why?
- *"It's a dumb theory and it doesn't have anything useful to say"*
- Roger has seen it used exactly **once** in 42 years of consulting

### The Loyalty Test
- At virtually every top business school (except possibly Harvard): if you don't swear allegiance to RBV, you have zero chance of being hired
- Roger couldn't teach his own approach at his own school when he was dean
- *"For the most part in the leading business schools, it's illegal to teach that"*

### The Strategy Landscape: Where Others Fit

| Thinker | Roger's Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Michael Porter** | The most important figure in strategy history; *Competitive Strategy* (1980) is landmark; wrongly caricatured by academics |
| **Hamilton Helmer** | Practical, non-academic; his 7 Powers fits into the "how to win" box — a categorical model of ways to win |
| **Richard Rumelt** | Academic who positioned himself against Porter; his "hyper-competition" framing misrepresents Porter; gave a lecture about P&G that had "zero to do with reality" |
| **Bruce Henderson** | Father of practical commercial strategy; founded BCG in 1963 |

## 📖 What Is Strategy?

> *"Strategy is an integrated set of choices that compels desired customer action."*

### The Key Insight
- Companies control: factories, R&D, advertising, hiring, pricing, etc.
- Companies do NOT control: what customers do
- *"The job of strategy is to make decisions on the things we do control that will compel — we can't force, but will compel — customers to take their hard-earned cash and give it to you"*

### Why "Integrated" Matters
- It's the **whole set of choices** working together
- Not one brilliant decision — the entire system must fit together and reinforce each other

## 🎯 The Strategy Choice Cascade: Five Questions

### The Origin
- Developed over 8 years (1987–1995) at Monitor Company
- Born from a real client problem: Porter's framework showed what good strategy looks like, but not how to get one
- *"Customers would ask us, 'We'd like to have one of those [good strategies]. How do we think through creating one?' We didn't actually have an answer."*

### The Five Questions

**1. What Is Your Winning Aspiration?**
- What are you trying to accomplish?
- Contextualizes the kinds of choices you could make
- Should be framed in terms of customer benefit, not market opportunity
- BAD: "It's a big market, we could get some of that"
- GOOD: "Our customers love what we do but think it's too narrow — if we could broaden it, they'd be very happy"

**2. Where Will We Play?**
- On what playing field will you compete?
- You cannot play everywhere — you must choose
- Dimensions of "where":
  - Which customers?
  - Which geographies?
  - Which distribution channels?
  - Which vertical stage of the value chain?
  - Finished product vs. component vs. integrator?

**3. How Will We Win?**
- Given where you've chosen to play, how will you be either:
  - **Better** at creating customer value than competitors (differentiation), OR
  - **Lower cost** than competitors
- *"There's no way to protect yourself if you're not one of those two"*

**4. What Capabilities Must We Have?**
- What capabilities do you need that competitors don't have?
- Must enable you to win in the way you've chosen
- Must be hard to replicate (either can't or won't)

**5. What Management Systems Are Required?**
- What enabling systems must be in place to build and maintain those capabilities?
- Recruiting, onboarding, career development, training, etc.
- *"The maintaining is an important part"*

### The Critical Rule
> *"You have to do them together."*

- Toggle back and forth between all five
- They must fit together and reinforce one another
- Changing one changes the others

## 🏆 Playing to Win vs. Playing to Play

### Signs You're Playing to Play (Not Winning)
- Customers can flip a coin between your product and a competitor's
- A competitor lowers their price and you can't match it without losing money
- You think you have the low-cost position but a competitor keeps pricing below you

### The Two Ways to Win

**Option 1: Differentiation**
- Customers specifically ask for your brand
- Example: Lego — kids say a store without Lego "is not a toy store"
- Lego has had 80-90% of entire category growth for most of the last decade
- Commands significant price premium over any competitor

**Option 2: Low Cost**
- You can always be at a sharper price point than competitors
- Example: Vanguard — $9 trillion AUM, lowest fees in the industry
- Requires dominant scale — you almost never win as a niche cost leader
- *"You have to keep expanding and expanding to get bigger"*

### What Happens If You're Neither
> *"It's literally like having a bully who can just shove you, and you take one step back, then they shove you, and you take another step back."*

- Competitors can jerk you around as much as they want
- You cannot bully Vanguard, Southwest, P&G, or Lego
- It's only a matter of time until you're dead

## ✈️ Southwest Airlines: The Perfect Case Study

### The Strategy
- **Where**: Point-to-point routes (not hub and spoke); secondary airports (Providence not Logan, Midway not O'Hare)
- **How**: Lowest cost position in the industry
- **Result**: Only airline to earn its cost of capital over 50 years; now #1 in passenger seat miles in America

### Why Competitors Can't Copy It
To replicate Southwest, you'd have to:
1. Sell off most of your aircraft fleet
2. Standardize on one aircraft type (737)
3. Tear up your entire hub-and-spoke route structure
4. Change your entire labor relations strategy (pay unions well for flexibility)
5. Fire all your travel agents
6. Convince customers to book online themselves

*"Life's too short. It's just too short."*

### The Counter-Positioning Insight
- Continental tried "Continental Lite" — did half the things Southwest does
- Result: a crappy Southwest
- *"The ultimate way to compete to win is to never actually be forced to compete"*

## 🏨 Four Seasons: Capabilities & Management Systems

### The Winning Insight
- High-end business travelers would rather be at home or the office than in a hotel
- *"We're going to define luxury as a service that makes up for what you left at home or at the office"*

### The Where to Play Choice (Value Chain)
- Got out of: real estate development, construction, hotel ownership
- Focused on: hotel management only
- Rich investors own the hotels; Four Seasons charges a management fee
- *"They do it with a stack this thick, we do it with a stack this thin"*

### The Capability Problem
- Hotel industry globally: **80% annual staff turnover**
- Average staff person is on a 16-month career at the hotel
- You cannot deliver customized, home-like service with that

### The Management System Solution
- Different recruiting, onboarding, career development
- Result: **10% turnover rate** — staff stay 10 years on average
- Staff can make decisions at a lower level without consulting a rule book
- *"This person just said, no, this is a good solution for my guest"*

### The Durability
- 35+ years of sustained competitive advantage
- Competitors (Mandarin Oriental, etc.) chose different "where" and "how" — didn't try to replicate
- *"The more nuanced and multifaceted your capabilities and management systems are, the more likely they're going to say life's too short"*

## 🔒 Capabilities, Moats & Competitive Advantage

### The Westlaw Example (Best Business Roger Has Seen)
- Dominant provider of online legal searches
- For 100+ years: every US legal case summarized by a Westlaw lawyer using proprietary keywords
- Today requires **1,500 full-time lawyers**
- Given free to law schools for 50 years — students learn it before graduating
- To replicate: hire 150,000 lawyers, create a competing keyword system, give it free to law schools for 50 years
- *"Nobody's even tried. Why bother? Life's too short."*

### The "Better" Trap
> *"Just being better is not a solution."*

- You have to answer: here's how I'm better AND here's why someone can't simply replicate that quickly
- The pet food internet example: anyone who can build a website can sell pet food online — 20 companies did immediately, all went bust
- Need capabilities that are hard to replicate: either **can't** or **won't**

### Can't vs. Won't
- **Can't**: Technically impossible or prohibitively expensive to replicate
- **Won't**: Competitor could do it but it would hurt their existing business too much

**Amazon vs. Walmart example:**
- Could Walmart have built as good a website as Amazon? Probably yes
- Did they? No — they hoped online wouldn't take off (it would hurt their 5,000 stores)
- Gave Amazon 10 years of scale advantage and network effects

**Tesla vs. OEMs:**
- GM created a fully functioning EV years ago
- Couldn't figure out how to make money on it → didn't do it
- Gave Tesla 10 years to establish brand and scale

### The Olay/Estée Lauder Counter-Positioning
- P&G repositioned Olay as a Clinique-like product in mass channels
- Estée Lauder could have killed this by bringing Clinique into mass channels
- Why didn't they? It would have destroyed their prestige channel relationships
- *"Their distribution channel would've killed them if they'd done it"*
- Result: Olay became the biggest brand in skincare; Clinique lost leadership

## 🌊 The "Water Finds Its Level" Principle

### On Google and AI Search
> *"I don't care how painful it is — water flows downhill, the tide comes in, and you cannot stop that, even if you're one of the most powerful three firms on the face of the planet."*

- Microsoft's Windows monopoly: last exciting update was Windows 95 (30 years ago)
- Microsoft's share of smartphone OS: 0.4%
- Share of tablet OS: ~4%
- Share of "minutes spent staring at a smart screen" has plummeted
- *"Water finds its own level — people said there are other ways of getting around this"*

### The Vanguard/ETF Lesson
- Jack Bogle didn't like ETFs — thought they weren't as good for customers as mutual funds
- But customers wanted them → Vanguard had to relent
- Now the leading index ETF provider AND index mutual fund provider
- *"He realized it was the tide, and he was attempting to hold back the tide"*

### The A.G. Lafley/Amazon Story
- A major retailer threatened to de-list P&G products if they kept cooperating with Amazon
- Lafley's response: *"If customers want to shop there, we just can't not be where our customers want to shop"*
- Offered the same terms to the threatening retailer
- The principle: you cannot hold back the customer tide

## 🔧 The FigJam Strategy Exercise (Live Example)

### Winning Aspiration
- BAD framing: "It's a big market, we could get some of that"
- BETTER framing: "Our core customers love Figma but think it's too narrow — if we could broaden it for them, they'd be very happy"
- *"Everything ties back to compelling desired customer action"*

### Where to Play
- Which customers: product managers, engineers, other functions
- Distribution: through existing Figma product (self-serve)
- Value chain position: feature within existing product vs. standalone product

### How to Win Options
- Lower cost than existing whiteboard tools
- Better integrated into workflow (don't have to leave your workflow to use it)
- AI-powered shortcuts (say a few words, it understands and builds the workflow)
- Better customer support for self-serve users

### Roger's Honest Assessment
- Starting from "big market, let's get some" is a weak foundation
- Starting from "customers are demanding more ways to work within Figma" is much stronger
- The strategy must be grounded in customer need, not market size

## 💡 Betterment Over Perfection: The Tactical Starting Point

### The Core Idea
> *"Don't try to solve the problems of the world, or even all the problems of your company. That's perfection. Betterment is making that gap go away."*

### How to Apply It
1. Identify your **most painful gap** — where current outcomes fall short of desired outcomes
2. Ask: what different choices could I make across the five questions?
3. Solve that one gap
4. Move to the next gap
5. Repeat

### The Professor of the Year Formula
- Taught 13 two-hour sessions per course
- After each course: polled students on every session
- Simply **cut the lowest-rated session** and replaced it with something new
- Did this for 25 years → course kept getting better and better
- Result: won Professor of the Year more times than anyone else
- *"Betterment doesn't make purists feel awesome, but I'm not here to make purists feel awesome"*

### Why This Works
- Current outcomes are a natural result of all the choices you've made
- Those outcomes won't improve much without different choices
- Focus on the single most painful gap → make it go away → repeat

## 🌟 The Procter & Gamble Lesson: Strategy at Every Level

### The P&G Insight
- 10% of S&P 500 CEOs are ex-P&G people
- Why? P&G understands that people **way down the organization** make super important strategic choices
- The Head and Shoulders brand franchise leader (4 levels below CEO) makes choices that determine whether the brand succeeds or fails
- *"If they don't make them well, the brand does terribly"*

### The Broader Principle
> *"People down the organization have to make really important strategic choices or bad things are going to happen. If they make really great ones, good things are going to happen, and they get trained to be a CEO someday."*

- Most companies: people at the top do strategy, people below do "execution"
- Roger hates the term "execution" for what people below the top do
- The P&G model is the minority view — but it produces the most CEOs

## 🏋️ Becoming a Great Strategist

### The Myth of the Natural Strategist
> *"I have never met this mythical beast called a great natural strategist."*

### What Great Strategists Actually Have in Common
- They just **practice**
- A.G. Lafley: practiced strategy in the Navy at age 25, decades before becoming CEO
- *"He just had more reps when he became CEO than almost anybody else I've ever met"*

### The Encouragement
- Anyone willing to practice strategy will get better
- There's no such thing as a person who practices strategy and stays "operational"
- *"The worst thing to do is to wait. Nobody who says 'I'll get to strategy later' ever amounts to anything."*
