## 🏢 One Key Lesson from Each Company

### Yahoo
- **The power of bundling under a single account**
- Yahoo ID was a powerful early innovation — first company to really pull off single-account bundling
- Google later did this extremely well (YouTube → Google account migration)
- Lesson: short-term pain (user backlash) can be right long-term — nobody remembers the YouTube account migration backlash now

### Google (6 years)
- **The power of thinking really, really big**
- Unwritten rule on ads team: *"If it's not going to generate more than $100 million in revenue, it's not worth talking about"*
- Regularly didn't pursue $100M opportunities because billion-dollar opportunities were available
- Downside: Google missed some trends because of this high bar — sometimes unclear if something is $100M or $1B
- Takeaway: even at Stripe, Shreyas was thinking much bigger about Stripe Connect because of this ingrained habit

### Twitter
- **The stickiness of combining network effects with core product differentiation**
- Twitter had both — and there's still no product quite like Twitter despite its age
- This combination creates extraordinary staying power even through infrastructure challenges and leadership issues

### Stripe
- **High energy + sound judgment + low ego + small teams = magic**
- Saw this combination more at Stripe than anywhere else
- *"Stripe wasn't by any means flawless, but I saw that combination more at Stripe than any other place I've been at"*

## 👤 One Key Lesson from Each Founder

### Larry Page (Google)
- **Strategic insight and ability to simulate the future to make better decisions**
- Particularly his ability to think ahead and use that to inform present decisions

### Jack Dorsey (Twitter)
- **Listening deeply, then asking one vital question**
- In meetings, Jack would listen far more than expected for a CEO
- Then ask one simple but penetrating question
- Example: During an acquisition discussion with all the pros/cons/metrics laid out, Jack asked: *"How does this make our users love Twitter more?"*
- Shreyas didn't have a good answer — realized they'd been talking about proxies, not the real thing
- Lesson: *"I still remember him asking that simple question. And frankly at the time, I did not have a good answer."*

### John Collison (Stripe)
- **Master marketer — talking about products in terms customers actually understand**
- Key insight: separate the effort involved in building something from the effort you put into talking about it
- Don't just talk about what you spent the most time building — talk about what resonates most with customers
- *"The features you talk about don't have to be the same features you spent the most time on"*

### Patrick Collison (Stripe)
- **Thinking and communicating with exceptional clarity**
- Setting culture by consistently being an example of the behaviors you want replicated
- *"Instead of talking about values, I saw both of them just live the values they wanted the company to replicate"*
- Consistency of behavior speaks louder than anything written on a poster

## 💡 Big Idea #1: Pre-Mortems

### The Core Insight
> *"If you do a pre-mortem right, you will not have to do an ugly post-mortem."*

- Inspired by observing the effectiveness of post-mortems → wondering: *"Why do we need to wait until after things go wrong?"*
- Discovered the concept from a **Harvard Business Review article by Gary Klein**

### The Genius: The Initial Prompt
Not just "what could go wrong?" — the specific framing is everything:

> *"Imagine this project has failed six months from now. It has miserably failed. Now let's work backwards — what went wrong? What could have contributed to this utter failure?"*

### Why It Works: Two Mechanisms

**1. Psychological Safety**
- Modern organizations reward optimism and positivity
- Nobody wants to be "the negative one"
- The pre-mortem framing gives everyone **license** to think about what can go wrong
- People leave the meeting smiling even after 30–60 minutes discussing terrible scenarios — it's cathartic

**2. Shared Vocabulary**
- Creates a common language for discussing threats
- In future meetings (even without Shreyas present), people started saying *"I have a tiger, can I bring this up?"*
- Makes it **okay** to surface concerns in any context

### The Three-Category Framework

| Category | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Tiger** | A real threat that could actually kill the project | A dependency that won't be ready in time |
| **Paper Tiger** | A seeming threat others worry about but you're not concerned about | Competitor launching something similar |
| **Elephant** | The elephant in the room nobody's talking about | *"We're assuming PR will drive users, but are we sure?"* |

### How to Run a Pre-Mortem: Step-by-Step

1. **Decide on scope**: Large launch = two separate pre-mortems (engineering/product + go-to-market). Small launch = one meeting with everyone
2. **Invite everyone**: Every engineer, PM, design, sales, support, marketing, PR — whoever is involved
3. **Share the prompt**: Leader opens with the failure scenario
4. **Silent individual work** (5–10 minutes): Everyone enters their tigers, paper tigers, elephants into a shared doc (Coda template) privately
5. **Round-robin sharing**: Go around the room, everyone shares
6. **Voting**: Each person picks the tiger that **someone else** mentioned that they find most scary (not their own)
7. **Post pre-mortem action plan**: Leader takes all output, prioritizes, shares with team, holds themselves accountable
8. **Key point**: Goal is not to solve every problem — it's to surface threats that aren't being openly discussed

### Real-World Impact
- Many public product failures (massive backlash, forced reversals) could have been prevented
- *"It is obvious to some team members, but they haven't created the psychological safety and vocabulary to talk about it objectively"*
- Pre-mortem is **low cost** (one meeting + some follow-up work) with very high upside
- Template available as a Coda doc (linked in show notes)

## 💡 Big Idea #2: The LNO Framework

### Origin Story
- First 3 years at Google: overwhelmed, stressed, working long hours, to-do list endless, couldn't sleep
- Discovered some ideas in a blog post → developed into the LNO framework
- Core realization: **not all tasks are created equal**

### The Three Task Types

| Type | Definition | Return on Effort |
|---|---|---|
| **L (Leverage)** | High-leverage tasks | Put in 1x, get 10x or 100x back |
| **N (Neutral)** | Standard tasks | Put in 1x, get ~1.1x back |
| **O (Overhead)** | Low-value tasks | Put in 1x, get back much less |

### The Core Problem
- Ambitious people and perfectionists **treat all three types the same**
- This is the root cause of overwhelm and dissatisfaction

### The Solution
- **L tasks**: Do them when most energetic/productive; let your inner perfectionist shine; spend more time than you normally would
- **N tasks**: Do them adequately — not perfectly
- **O tasks**: Do them minimally — just enough

### Key Insight: Same Activity, Different Categories
The same type of task can be L, N, or O depending on context:

**Filing a bug report:**
- Critical bug affecting core revenue feature → **L task** (detailed, annotated screenshots, thorough description)
- Minor cosmetic bug → **O task** (one screenshot, hit submit)

**Taking meeting notes:**
- CEO product review on contentious strategic decision → **L task** (perfect the language, ensure no misinterpretation, spend 1–2 hours)
- Standard team standup → **O task** (send immediately after meeting, just list 3 action items and owners)

**Expense reports:**
- Always an **O task** — Shreyas used to try to make them really good. This made no sense.

### How to Identify Your L Tasks
> *"PMs implicitly know what their L tasks are because those are the tasks that are bothering them the most — because they're not doing them or not doing them as well as they know they should."*

Classic L task that gets avoided: **strategy work**
- *"I know I need to work on strategy, but I'm busy firefighting. Next month I'll have time."*
- Next month: same thing
- Real reason for procrastination: **fear** — fear that you won't have anything interesting to say, fear you can't formulate a good strategy
- Time becomes a convenient excuse

### Two Tactics for Tackling L Tasks

**1. Placebo Productivity**
- In the days leading up to an L task, intentionally fill your day with N and O tasks
- This tricks your brain: *"I've been doing neutral/overhead tasks for two days — now it's time for the real thing"*
- Gives you psychological permission to shift into deep work mode

**2. Change of Location**
- On the appointed day for an L task, work from somewhere different (coffee shop, coworking space)
- The change of environment forces focus and a shift in mindset
- *"Nothing fights my procrastination for L tasks better than changing the place from which I'm working"*

## 💡 Big Idea #3: The Three Levels of Product Work

### The Three Levels

| Level | Focus | Who Defaults Here |
|---|---|---|
| **Impact** | What is the effect on users, brand, business? | CEOs, founders |
| **Execution** | How do we get this done? What's the next milestone? | PMs, ICs |
| **Optics** | How do we create awareness of what we're doing? | Varies |

### The Classic Conflict: A Concrete Example
- PM presenting to CEO at a product review
- CEO asks: *"Why is our customer service response time going to be so high?"*
- PM has a rational answer: VP of Customer Support doesn't have funding this quarter, but next quarter they'll allocate more people
- Rest of the review doesn't go well
- **Why**: CEO is thinking at the **impact level** (what's the impact to customer experience and brand?); PM is thinking at the **execution level** (how do we hit the next milestone given constraints?)
- Neither is wrong — they're just defaulting to different levels
- *"We litigate the minutiae without recognizing there's a fundamental mismatch in levels"*

### Optics: The Nuanced View
> *"Optics is about creating awareness of the impact and the execution that you're doing or your team is doing."*

**Why optics matters (internal):**
- Creates energy
- Creates awareness
- Creates excitement
- Creates opportunities for feedback
- Enables greater impact and better execution

**When optics becomes a problem:**
- When it becomes the **goal** rather than a means to an end
- Organizations signal this through who they hire, fire, promote, and what they celebrate at all-hands
- If the CEO regularly appreciates status updates (an optics activity) more than results, the signal is: focus on optics
- *"The organization didn't wake up and say 'this is the culture we want.' It just happens through little actions every day."*

### Practical Application: Match Level to Context

| Situation | Recommended Default Level |
|---|---|
| Early-stage startup | **Execution** — you have a hypothesis, now convert it to product |
| Platform team with reliability issues | **Optics** — communicate better with dependent teams |
| Mature product with clear strategy | **Impact** — focus on outcomes, not just shipping |

### Organizational Implications
- What level an organization promotes for tells you a lot about its culture
- Does it promote people who default to impact? Execution? Optics?
- This has wide-ranging effects on how the organization functions

## 💡 Big Idea #4: Most Execution Problems Are Strategy (or Culture) Problems

### The Core Observation
> *"Most execution problems that I encounter in a high-performing environment where everybody has the right intentions are actually not execution problems. They are either strategy problems, interpersonal problems, or cultural problems."*

### The Classic Scenario: Team Misalignment
- Two teams need to work together but are misaligned
- Affects both teams' ability to hit OKRs
- Leader does a dozen meetings, diagnoses the issue, creates a new review process, sets up regular syncs
- One month later: same problem, different manifestation

**Why the bandaid doesn't work:**

**If it's a strategy problem:**
- Teams are misaligned because they don't know what the strategy is
- OKRs were crafted without a clear strategy → not well-aligned
- No shared sense of priorities or how to respond when reality changes
- *"Nobody recognizes that we could fix this if we had a clear strategy"*

**If it's a culture problem:**
- Culture optimizes for individual OKR achievement
- Team A can't help Team B because their manager will get their wrist slapped for missing OKRs
- No amount of syncs will fix a culture that punishes cross-team collaboration

**If it's an interpersonal problem:**
- The two team managers simply don't get along
- Creating friction constantly
- Solution: coach them through their differences — when solved, you won't need the monthly review meeting

### When IS It Actually an Execution Problem?
Real execution problems do exist:

1. **Infrastructure issues**: Old infrastructure can't sustain usage → causes slowdowns, outages
2. **Skills gap**: Engineers not skilled in required technology; PM who's a zero-to-one person assigned to a scale/mature initiative

### The Surefire Diagnostic: The Bandaid Test
> *"When you put on a bandaid and the bandaid falls — that's how you know it's not really an execution problem."*

- Organizations constantly solving the same problem over and over
- Organizational memory is surprisingly short — forget that the bandaid from 3 months ago didn't work
- *"Oh, let's create a new solution" → new meeting → same problem resurfaces*

### The Whack-a-Mole Pattern
- Leaders in high-growth environments are overwhelmed
- Two reasons: (1) high optics requirements, (2) constantly solving execution problems
- Solve the most important ones → new ones appear → solve those → two more appear
- This cycle never ends if the underlying strategy/culture/interpersonal issue isn't addressed

## 💡 Big Idea #5: Minimize Opportunity Cost, Not Maximize ROI

### The Core Shift
> *"Stop doing work that simply provides a positive ROI. Start focusing on work that minimizes opportunity cost."*

### Why ROI Thinking Is Suboptimal in High-Leverage Roles

**The ROI Formula:**
`ROI = (Value Created - Cost of Time) / Cost of Time`

**The Problem:**
- Time is in the **denominator**
- To maximize ROI, you naturally try to **decrease the denominator** (spend less time per task)
- This leads to: prioritizing low-hanging fruit, quick wins, well-defined tasks
- *"We fill up our plate with quick wins"*
- Result: you're busy, you feel productive, but you're missing the big opportunities

**The Opportunity Cost Formula:**
`Opportunity Cost = Value of Optimal Option - Value of Chosen Option`

**Why This Is Better:**
- Forces you to ask: *"Is this the best use of my time?"* not just *"Is this a good use of my time?"*
- Subtle but profound shift
- You will pick things you would never have picked with an ROI mindset

### Real Example: Stripe Connect
- When Shreyas joined, team was working on lots of positive ROI things
- He noticed customers had a growing need for more flexible marketplace payment management
- *"We wouldn't have looked at it if we were just focused on positive ROI"*
- Shifted mindset → realized it was a huge opportunity → pursued it → became a major product and large business for Stripe

### The Planning Manifestation
- Most common place this shows up: quarterly/annual planning
- Five well-defined, sure-thing, positive-ROI items vs. two ambiguous but potentially transformative items
- Teams almost always pick the five sure things
- *"One of those two ambiguous things could meaningfully change the trajectory of your business"*

### Practical Implementation: The Allocation Framework
Don't try to quantify opportunity cost (it's a lost cause). Instead:

**Give teams a rough time allocation as guidance:**

Inspired by Google's 70/20/10:
- **60%** → Incremental features that improve users' lives day-to-day (high ROI, well-defined)
- **30%** → Big new initiatives (1–2 max, given the 30% constraint)
- **10%** → Stability and infrastructure

**Why this works:**
- Teams know they have 30% for big bets → no sense filling that bucket with more quick wins
- Creates permission and space to pursue transformative work
- *"I often get surprised with all the awesome stuff they come back with"*

## 🎁 Bonus Big Idea: High Agency

### The Definition
Coined by Eric Weinstein; Shreyas's version:
> *"High agency is about finding a way to get what you want without waiting for conditions to be perfect or otherwise blaming the circumstances."*

High agency people either:
1. **Push through** adverse conditions, OR
2. **Reverse** the adverse conditions to achieve their goals

### Why It's Especially Important for PMs
PMs constantly face adverse conditions:
- Not enough resources
- Legacy infrastructure challenges
- Staffing issues
- Customer problems
- Organizational friction

### The Three Components of High Agency

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Ownership mindset** | Taking strong ownership regardless of circumstances |
| **Creative execution** | Finding creative ways through challenges |
| **Resilience** | Maintaining momentum through setbacks |

### The Two PM Archetypes

**Overperformer**: Credentials look modest on paper → impact far exceeds expectations
- Despite disadvantages, takes strong ownership
- Creatively executes through challenges
- High resilience

**Underperformer**: Tremendous potential on paper → nowhere near achieving it
- Waits for conditions to be right
- Attributes lack of progress to circumstances

### Why This Resonated
*"It gave vocabulary to people for what they already kind of understood and had seen, but did not have the words for."*
