## 🏆 What Separates a Top 1% PM: The Full List

### The Original List (from the post)
1. **Think Big** — always hunting for bigger impact
2. **Communicate** — a test of thinking AND communication
3. **Simplify** — do more with less, higher impact through simplification
4. **Prioritize** — the core skill of a PM after communication
5. **Forecast and Measure** — what separates PMs from consultants; forecast → execute → measure → build instincts
6. **Execute** — ship what you said you'd ship
7. **Understand Technical Trade-offs** — don't need to be an engineer, but understand trade-offs
8. **Understand Good Design** — think in the mind of a customer/user
9. **Write Effective Copy** — communicate with customers in a few words; don't outsource this

### The Additions (Added in Newsletter Refresh)
10. **Earn Trust with Others** — the currency of a product manager and product leader
11. **Dig for Data** — it's out there; develop tools to find it, don't depend on analysts or existing reports
12. **Push Back Effectively** — an art and skill; ability to do this correlates with ability to grow as a leader
13. **Adapt to Change** — how you react impacts your mood, morale, and ability to rally yourself and team
14. **Driven by Impact, Not Promotion** — wake up thinking about impact; promotion will follow

### The Important Caveat
> *"I've never met a one percent PM that does all these things."*

No one is the best at everything. This is a framework for growth, not a checklist for perfection.

## 🆕 Top 3 Skills for New PMs

### Ian's Answer: Communicate, Prioritize, Execute

**1. Communicate**
- The most important skill at every level — stakes just get higher as you grow
- The Microsoft story: manager asked "when is this going to ship?" — Ian gave background and context but didn't answer the question
- The Amazon story: worked for Kim Rachmeler (Amazon's first TPM, SVP for Worldwide Discovery) — learned from her feedback
- Started "The Book of Kim" with his first boss Russell — gathered best practices:
  - Avoid weasel words
  - Answer first, then explain
  - Own your problems
- Later expanded into "The Operator's Manual" post

**The Simple Tactical Tip:**
> *"Answer first, then explain. Or sometimes answer, then shut up."*

- Grade yourself after every communication: how could I have done that better?
- Ask your manager: "What could I have done better in how I communicated in that last meeting?"
- This continuous improvement mindset compounds over time

**2. Prioritize**
- *"The number one key tool of a product manager"*
- Multiple dimensions:
  - Which themes to prioritize in a roadmap
  - Which projects within a theme
  - How to sequence projects
  - How much of a project to build
  - Time management (what to spend your time on)
  - Which things to starve for attention

> *"Given the same amount of skill, intelligence, and resources, a product manager with a great innate ability to prioritize is going to generate 5x the impact of someone without that skill."*

- Ian's early success at Amazon wasn't from being smarter or more technical — it was from being hungry for impact and honing in on the highest-leverage projects

**3. Execute**
- Mold what you want to build into a simple, compact package with highest impact
- Execution is a function of the team: designers, data scientists, engineers
- You have ownership in helping the team get better at every sprint
- *"Product managers are the motive power behind execution and impact"*
- If you stall, the project stalls

## 🎯 Top 3 Skills for Senior PMs

### Ian's Answer: Communicate (Still), Think Big, Earn Trust, Driven by Impact

**1. Communicate (Still)**
- Stakes are higher at senior levels
- *"That's the one at every level of product — it's just the stakes are higher"*

**2. Think Big**
- Warren Buffett: *"At our scale, we've got to hunt for bigger elephants"*
- For any idea or solution: take a minute at the beginning and ask "could this be bigger?"
- Start small but with a bigger vision in mind
- Expand your scope beyond just "product or tech" — own anything that influences your product's success
- *"You own it until you find somebody else to own it"*
- Think like an owner: think beyond your one little product bubble

**3. Earn Trust**
> *"Trust is truly the currency of a product leader — probably any leader in any function."*

- If leadership doesn't trust you to use resources well, you won't get more resources
- Trust is built by **repeatedly setting and meeting expectations**
- *"Call your shots — forecast, set a goal, hit it. If you do that repeatedly, you're going to be in pretty good shape."*

**Practices that BUILD trust:**
- Tell the truth without fail
- Launch when you said you'd launch
- Launch what you said you'd launch
- Own your mistakes
- Have high standards for yourself

**Practices that LOSE trust:**
- Lying or being evasive
- Not shipping what you said you'd ship
- Ignoring mistakes or repeating them

**The Airbnb Trust Story (Ian's Own Failure):**
- Brought in to build customer support technology platform the Amazon way
- Was heads-down executing what he thought was right
- Didn't spend enough time partnering with the customer support leadership team
- *"I maybe hadn't spent as much time and effort on that as I thought to get that true support there such that that leader would rally the organization around it"*
- Lesson: *"The right product in the right direction and the right strategy may not land if you don't have that support"*

**4. Driven by Impact, Not Promotion**
> *"If you forget about everything else — forget about politics, forget about promotion or having a bigger org — if you simply wake up every day trying to have the biggest impact you can, that's a really good guiding light."*

- Ian's first 10 years at Amazon: never talked to his manager about promotion, just focused on growing his business
- Net result: promoted several times
- Impact drives promotion; promotion shouldn't drive impact

## 🔄 The Working Backwards Process: Deep Dive

### What Working Backwards Actually Is
> *"Working backwards is all about the problem — starting there and obsessing about the problem, and being guided by it to then go into the solution."*

The press release and FAQ are **mechanisms** to enforce working backwards — they're not the core of it. The core is:
1. Start with the customer problem
2. Be faithful to that problem
3. Work backwards to the solution

### The #1 Mistake Teams Make

**They don't actually work backwards — they start with the solution:**
- "We have these two technologies, we could combine them"
- "We have these ingredients in the pantry, let's make a meal"
- Then they retrofit the customer and the problem after the fact

> *"If you start talking about the technology or the building blocks, that's the likely case that you're not really working backwards."*

**The Amazon Smile origin story (Ian's own mistake):**
- There was a "Jeff idea" the team was excited about called "ASIN to ASIN Linking"
- The name itself was the tell — it was technology-first, not customer-first
- They wrote the press release and did the process, but weren't truly working backwards
- Result: it wasn't successful; Ian shut it down
- *"I learned a lot about using the working backwards process but not really working backwards"*

### The Working Backwards Mechanism: The Internal Press Release

**Structure:**
1. **Problem paragraph** — what problem are you solving for the customer?
2. **Solution paragraph** — how does your product solve that problem?
3. **Customer quote** — what would a customer say about this?
4. **The FACT** — is there a legitimate plan to succeed?

**The Jeff Bezos lesson on the problem paragraph:**
- Ian once came with a press release that skipped the problem paragraph
- Bezos: *"Maybe if you don't have a problem paragraph, there's not really a problem"*
- Ian realized he'd had the solution in mind and skipped over the problem — not truly working backwards

### The FACT: The Underrated Part

**Jeff Bezos's three criteria for investing in something new (from a lunch with Ian ~2008):**
1. Is it a big idea?
2. Is it something we should be doing? (e.g., a new way to extract oil from shale might be a big idea, but not for Amazon)
3. Is there a legitimate plan to succeed?

> *"The FACT basically says: I've thought through the internal components, the finances, the key technical hurdles, and so this thing has a plan."*

- The FACT is the early-stage test of whether the idea deserves resources
- It's about building trust that you've been thoughtful enough to move forward

### How to Implement Working Backwards Outside Amazon

**The spirit vs. the mechanism:**
- The spirit: always start with the customer problem, be faithful to it
- The mechanism: the press release + FACT format

**Practical advice for product leaders:**
- You have more leverage with your team than with leadership above you
- With your team: use documents, build the muscle of working backwards
- With leadership: find opportunities to try it, ask "are you open to reviewing a doc instead of slides?"
- Acknowledge the environment — different leaders process information differently
- *"Unless the organization has a specific format, if it does you probably just need to use it"*

**When to use the full working backwards process:**
- Significant new products: absolutely
- Small features below a certain scale: probably not necessary (overhead isn't worth it)
- The spirit should always be there; the mechanism is optional

### Signs You're NOT Actually Working Backwards

**Red flag #1:** You're talking about building blocks/ingredients
- "These two technologies look similar, we could combine them"
- "We have these assets, we could put them together"

**Red flag #2:** You're excited about what you could build before you've identified a compelling problem
- The opposite of this: a customer problem that feels compelling even before you know the solution

**The pantry metaphor:**
> *"In a big company, you have more ingredients in the pantry. You have all these ideas because you have more ways you could combine them and try to feed it to someone. But that may not be working backwards."*

## 🏢 Lessons from Amazon, Bezos & Jeff Wilkie

### Why People Stay at Amazon So Long
- Amazon quickly reveals whether you're a fit
- Ian: *"I'm kind of a structured thinker and right-brained, and this idea that I could have this business and figure out one metric that determines success and my job is to make that number go up and to the right — that fit with me"*
- Continuous learning from smart, driven people
- Reading documents (not slides) forces deeper thinking
- Weekly Business Reviews (WBR) with Jeff — learning from everyone in the room, not just your own experience

### Jeff Bezos: Encouraging, Not Terrifying
- Contrary to the rhetoric: Ian found Bezos super encouraging
- Each meeting ended with encouragement even if they didn't find the answer
- Quote from Bezos: *"This process doesn't have to be efficient, because the prerequisite for efficiency is knowing where you're going"*
- Bezos used these reviews to teach the working backwards process, not just approve/reject

### Jeff Wilkie: The Consummate Operator
**Three key attributes Ian learned from Wilkie:**

**1. The Operator Mindset**
- Wilkie developed Amazon's operational muscle
- The WBR mechanism: review metrics for the entire North American retail business in one hour
- Leaders had to know their business cold — speak to variances and trends in key metrics
- Cascading effect: Ian would do the same with his team, who would do the same with theirs

**2. Tough Love (Done Right)**
- Professional and respectful, but willing to give the "look over the glasses" moment
- *"Sometimes everyone needs a little kick in the ass"*
- This built more respect, not less

**3. Teaching the Why**
> *"It wasn't just saying 'this is the right thing' — it was teaching the why, the mental model that informs him to think this is the right thing."*

- Taking 10 seconds in a meeting to teach the underlying principle
- This has a lasting effect: the person learns the lesson and can apply it to future situations
- Also helps people "disagree and commit" more genuinely because they understand the reasoning

## 💡 The Impact-First Flywheel

### How It Works for Junior PMs
- Junior PMs: impact is a "nice to have" — you're mostly executing assigned projects
- But: work on things that are likely to have impact even if you're not setting strategy
- Choose offense over defense: work on things that drive dollars or customers, not just cost reduction
- Who you work for matters enormously: a great manager is a better teacher and role model

### How It Works for Senior PMs
- Senior PMs: impact is how you're evaluated
- Two people, same level: one has massive impact, one doesn't → the first person should grow and get bigger challenges
- *"If you're impactful at a bigger challenge, that's even more important for the business"*

### The Flywheel
Impact → Bigger challenges → More resources → More impact → Promotion (as a byproduct)
