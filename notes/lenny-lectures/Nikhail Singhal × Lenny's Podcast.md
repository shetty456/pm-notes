## 🐇 The Greyhound Dog Track Metaphor (Opening & Closing Frame)
- Growing up in the Midwest, entertainment was going to **greyhound dog tracks**
- Dogs were motivated by **fake mechanical rabbits** running faster than them around the track
- When machines broke and dogs **actually caught the rabbit** → they **never ran again**
- Reason: *"What's next? I've achieved what I was looking for."*
- **The career parallel:** Most people spend their careers chasing a North Star (VP title, more money, starting a company) but never think about what comes after achieving it
- When they get there, their entire wiring — built around reaching that goal — has nothing left to run toward
- Result: sadness, creating unnecessary conflict, bad habits, spending money to fill the void

## 📍 ACT ONE: EARLY CAREER

### The Core Mistake — Short-Term Thinking
- Most common early career error: making decisions based on **immediate pain** rather than long-term direction
- Common short-term triggers people use to justify moves:
  - "I really dislike my boss"
  - "This company doesn't have it anymore"
  - "It's too hard to ship things"
- Nikhail's counter: *"These statements are probably true in many of the jobs you'd consider moving to. Lateral moves are by definition not forward moves."*

### The "Skip" Framework — Think Two Jobs Ahead
- The newsletter/podcast name "The Skip" comes from this core idea
- Don't think about your next job — think about **the job after next**
- Don't think about your boss's job — think about **your boss's boss's job**
- Work backwards from your end state, like building a product:
  - Great product → break into v1, v2, v3
  - Great career → what does the end state look like? Work backward from there
- Example: If you want to found a company one day, look at your current job and next job **in service of that goal** — which may mean staying and learning to deal with ambiguity rather than leaving

### Promotion ≠ Career
- Biggest conflation in early careers: **promotion and career advancement are not the same thing**
- When people say "I want a career talk," they almost always mean "how do I get promoted faster?"
- Promotion is the **company's system** for recognizing forward movement — it's short-term
- Career is the **long-term arc** — the job after next, the skill you're building, the story you're telling
- Classic symptom: *"As soon as I get promoted, I'm going to leave."* → Then what was the promotion in service of?

### The Logo-Chasing Problem
- Collecting brand-name logos (Netflix, Meta, Airbnb, Uber) **feels shallow to most builders**
- People got into product because they like **building things** — not collecting labels
- Nikhail's preferred frame: collect **diverse experiences**, not logos:
  - Pre-product market fit (learning to find it)
  - Smoke turning into fire (witnessing/shepherding PMF)
  - Fire turning into something great (scaling)
  - Inside-the-building problems (org/execution)
  - Outside-the-building problems (consumer vs. B2B)
- *"The more diverse career you have, the better builder you are."*
- Chasing logos in service of a future dream that isn't build-oriented → leads to sadness

### When FAANG/MAGMA Companies Actually Matter
- Nikhail prefers **MAGMA** over FAANG (Facebook → Meta, so F is outdated; also excludes Adobe, Salesforce)
- Logo value is **especially important for executives** — not necessarily for ICs
- Why: executives are hired to bring expertise of the **next phase** of an organization
- MAGMA companies have specific expertise in: building at scale, managing millions/billions of users
- Being successful there = an endorsement that's hard to substitute
- Caveat: can be substituted by other late-stage companies, but if you've **never experienced scale**, it's very hard to be a C-level exec at a growth company

### Two Goals for Early PMs

**Goal 1: Pick a lane and become world-class in one type of ambiguity**

Five types of product ambiguity:
| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Craft ambiguity** | World-class at the craft of building products |
| **Market ambiguity** | Understanding how to navigate markets, create something new |
| **Organizational ambiguity** | Taking complex teams with complex goals and solving inside-the-building problems |
| **Domain expertise** | ML expert, hardware expert, specific vertical knowledge |
| **Team/management expertise** | Thriving in managing managers, getting the balance right |
| *(Bonus)* **Growth** | Understanding growth mechanics deeply |

- Pick **one** as early as possible
- Being a PM means you're confronted with all five — but you need a primary lane

**Goal 2: Build a story to tell**
- Think like Amazon's press release process — write the story **before** you start
- In interviews, the failure mode is people who were "just there" — can't articulate what they learned or did
- *"Think about the story, think about the skill, then solve your day-to-day, week-to-week, month-to-month."*
- Use **"I"** in the story, not "we" — own your specific contribution

## 📍 ACT TWO: MID-CAREER

### Ex-Growth Companies — The Hidden Career Trap

**What is an ex-growth company?**
- Companies that raised substantial capital during the **blitzscaling era** (last 10 years)
- When zero-interest-rate environment ended (~18 months before recording), growth through capital became much harder
- These companies: **not running out of money** (long runway), **not laying off visibly**, sometimes still hiring
- But: their contemporary public companies are worth **10% or less** of peak valuations
- They're **privately held** → hiding in the shadows, no public market signal

**The two diagnostic questions:**
1. Are we **scaling a product** with customers who love us and a "tremendous sucking sound" of demand?
2. Or are we **still trying to find** that product-market fit?

If still searching for PMF **and** your valuation is hundreds of millions or more → **you're an ex-growth company**

**Why it's dangerous for employees:**
- Half or more of compensation is in equity
- That equity is likely worth very little or nothing
- Effective pay cut of 50%+ vs. market rate
- *"The opportunity cost is just too rich."*
- Nikhail's advice: **leave**. As an employee you have almost no recourse — it's essentially a new 4-year investment to stay

**Founder options (more but harder):**
- Recap the company
- Reset stock price, reissue equity
- Return some capital to board and continue
- Pull the plug and restart
- Founders have more to lose but also more levers

**Signs of no product-market fit:**
- How hard are you working to acquire users?
- Is there a **"sucking pull"** — people coming in the door with minimal marketing effort?
- OpenAI example: ridiculous pull even without massive revenue/profitability yet
- Early signal is qualitative pull, not unit economics (those come later)

**Reasons to potentially stay:**
1. It's the **biggest role** you could get — career-additive executive experience
2. **Loyalty** to the team (respect this, but put bounds on it)
- **Fear of finding another job** = bad reason, but very common

### Why People Don't Get Promoted — The Four Real Reasons

**Reason 1: No Advocate**
- You need someone to **see the magic in you** — doesn't have to be your direct manager
- If you have the magic but are in a bad setting (manager/promotion committee doesn't see it) → change the setting
- Options: shift within the project, find a manager who sees it, leave the company

**Reason 2: The Role Doesn't Exist**
- Especially acute now with layoffs and manager layer reductions
- Someone can be performing at the next level but if the **job doesn't exist**, there's nothing to promote into
- Very different from being unqualified — the system just isn't positioned to advocate

**Reason 3: Impatience**
- Highest performers are used to being promoted faster than average
- Leadership roles take **longer to absorb** — more soft skills, more subtle, impact is often lagging
- If you're used to annual promotions and now you're in a leadership role and not moving as fast: *"That may be working as intended."*

**Reason 4: Real Development Area (The Hardest One)**
- ~50% of cases: there IS a substantive development area
- Failure modes:
  - Manager is poor at identifying it
  - Promotion committee sees it but manager doesn't surface it
  - Individual **refuses to hear it**
  - Individual hears it but **doesn't want to change** — not arrogance, but identity: *"This is who I am. You want me to be X and I'm Y."*
- This is where coaching, development, and self-awareness come in

### Getting Real Feedback (Not Formal Feedback)
- Formal feedback systems at scale companies produce **dramatically different signals** than real conversations
- What you want: **ground truth** of what people are actually thinking
- Key skills:
  - Strong **listener skills** — don't immediately justify or explain away feedback
  - **Triangulate** from people who see you often AND people who don't
  - Create **psychological safety** — no fear of retaliation
- Jules Walters technique (referenced): ask for feedback, and no matter how much you're "melting inside," just say *"Thank you so much for that"*
- Nikhail's upgrade on that: **repeat the feedback back better than it was given** — shows you internalized it, makes the giver feel safe to share more
- Manager tip: when someone gives you feedback, **name them publicly in your next staff meeting** and credit them for the insight → signals that giving feedback gets recognized → breaks down the barrier

### Why Managers Are Often Not Great
- Industry assumption: *"If you're a good coder, you're ready to manage. If you can product manage, you should definitely tell people what to do."*
- The analogy: *"If you can make a good car, you must know how to make the factory that makes the car."* — Nikhail says this is completely false
- Tech industry has massively under-invested in **manager training** — unlike the military or other mature industries
- Result: a "massive epidemic" with thousands of downstream challenges (bias, coaching failures, development area blindness)
- Nikhail's take: *"Your podcast might be more meaningful than most L&D departments in most organizations today."*

### Two Things New Managers Must Get Right

**1. Share the Steering Wheel**

Three wrong mental models:
- "Divide and conquer" — you go there, I go here, we meet up
- "Teaching to ride a bike" — I hold your hand, let go, hope you fly

The right model: **Sidecar on a motorcycle**
- The person is driving the motorcycle; you're in the sidecar
- You're attached whether you like it or not
- Your role is relatively specific: **give counsel**
- Not "I got it," not "you got it," not "I'll hand it to you later" — it's shared, ongoing

**2. Earn the Right to Manage — Be Invited In**

- Having organizational power ≠ having earned the right to manage
- You're like a **vampire at the door** — you have to be invited in
- First question to ask: *"What can I help you with?"*
- Common response: *"I don't need you. I wasn't excited about you as a manager."* → That's fine. Anything you say after that will backfire
- Wait in the sidecar. One day they will need help.
- When invited in: **pick one or two specific areas** to partner on — don't try to manage everything at once
- The sequence: invitation → pick specific area → share responsibility

### The Senior IC Path
- Nikhail calls it **one of the best things to happen to the industry**
- Why it's emerging now: management was perceived as the way to drive expansion during blitzscaling; now companies are doing fewer things → IC track is growing
- The problem with the old path: promising ICs were promoted into management early, didn't get trained, became average managers with mediocre stories
- The IC advantage in hiring: *"I've been an IC the whole time. I went from learning → demonstrating → taking it forward. I mastered one of these ambiguities."* → That's exactly what a growth company needs
- Engineering and design already had this (VP of Engineering vs. CTO; design managers vs. craft designers) — product management is finally catching up
- The IC track is still "tender" — not in cement yet — but Nikhail predicts it will solidify significantly in the next 6–9 months
- Prediction: *"Builder and IC will become synonymous."*
- Airbnb note: renamed Product Management to **Product Marketing** — PMs are now product marketers — because Brian Chesky believes your job is also to make sure people use what you build

### Meeting Operating System — The Underrated Lever
- At scale, the **meeting operating system is as important as the products you're building**
- Nikhail runs his teams on a **versioned meeting system** — currently on version 7
- Every 90 days: review all meetings like a product — attendees, cadence, decision-making process, hybrid/WFH structure
- Takes feedback 2 months in, makes another rev every 3 months
- Benefits: people can plan, meeting time becomes effective, decisions get made at the right level
- *"Meeting time is the most expensive time in a company."*
- For new leaders with low context: **this is the one thing you can do immediately** — you can see inefficiencies that people inside the system can't see
- Nikhail's leadership sequence: **Process first → People → Product → Strategy**

## 📍 ACT THREE: LATE CAREER / CPOs

### Shadows of Superpowers — The Senior Leader Blind Spot
- Concept from a therapist: *"Everyone focuses on your superpowers, but no one ever thinks about what shadows they create."*
- Related adage: *"What got you here isn't what got you there"* — the tools that made you successful need to be rebuilt for the next phase

**Common superpower → shadow pairings:**

| Superpower | Shadow |
|---|---|
| Great collaborator | Reticent to present own opinions; struggles with senior cross-functional stakeholders who need you to be opinionated |
| Amazing at growth | Struggles to be innovative |
| World-class storyteller | Struggles to get into the details |
| Strong taste-maker / first to have a POV | Doesn't introduce change often enough |
| Strong politically | Decisions are unprincipled |
| Structured thinker | Blue-sky innovation is very tough |
| Amazing listener | Very weak at being decisive |

- The trap: you go into a performance review, someone says you struggle with collaboration, and you say *"Are you kidding me? My last five reviews said I was one of the best collaborators in the company."*
- What happened: you were collaborating as long as people agreed with you. Now as a leader, you need to be **opinionated** — and you're using the exact same toolset that no longer fits
- Nikhail's diagnostic superpower: *"Just knowing what I know about the person, I can unlock their development area faster than anyone — because I'll bet on what their shadows are based on their strengths."*
- The hard part: it's their **identity**. *"This is what got me here. If you make me work on that, you'll make me change my superpower."*
- Once people have the **name and the face** of their shadow, almost everyone can solve it. Getting to the name is the hard part.

**Nikhail's own example:**
- Superpower: very opinionated, used small amounts of information to make decisions, great at driving things → classic entrepreneur/grit profile
- Shadow: as an executive, you need to **edit your opinions for the collective**, ensure everyone has context, be heard — but as someone who's "been right a lot," this felt like a complete identity rewire
- Turning point: direct feedback from **someone he trusted** who gave it for the right reasons with the right language — after dismissing the same feedback from others for a long time
- Lesson: *"I had a lot of feedback that I was dismissing. Then I had feedback from someone I'm like — that person I should listen to."*

**How to catch your own shadow:**
- Go back through all your feedback and look at the **discard pile** — things you dismissed as anomalies
- Those discarded items are often artifacts of your strength being in the way
- Managers often do the discarding for you: *"Oh, that person had it out for you, they were just upset."* → Nikhail says: *"No. Perception is reality. Tell me about that one."*
- Don't need a coach — you need to **listen to contradictory feedback** without dismissing it
- Recognize: you're probably doing it correctly — you've just gotten to the next level where the same tool doesn't fit

**Company-level parallel:**
- Meta: "Move fast and break things" → became the biggest Achilles heel
- Uber: similar
- Airbnb: similar
- The same superpower → shadow dynamic applies to companies, relationships, and individuals

### Mental Health at the Top — The Untold Story
- Nikhail: *"This is a story that isn't told very well right now — partly because it's such a luxury problem."*
- Pattern: people succeed → then become **lost** — the two go hand in hand
- Career is now potentially **60 years long** (people working into their 70s/80s due to desk work and better health)
- Even if you're 20–30 years in, you're only **halfway through**
- Act Three (post-peak achievement) is longer than ever but we don't talk about it enough

**What happens when people "catch the rabbit":**
- Their entire wiring — built around reaching the North Star — has nothing left to run toward
- Outcomes: sadness, creating unnecessary conflict ("creating war when peace is needed"), bad spending habits, destructive behaviors
- *"You'll keep working because you don't know anything else to do, but you'll be sadder."*

**Lenny's personal example:**
- Started a company, sold to Airbnb, got there and thought: *"What the hell do I do now? I don't have any other goals."*
- Nikhail's reframe: Lenny reinvented himself by finding a new North Star (giving, content, community) that he can do for 30 years — with many spokes to it

**Two categories of Act Three North Stars:**

1. **Scaled economics** — making tens into hundreds, hundreds into more; entrepreneurship → investing → private equity
   - Nikhail is neutral on whether this is good or bad — it's a personal decision

2. **Giving** — Eastern philosophies have talked about this for thousands of years as the end state of happiness
   - Acts 1 and 2: okay to be in "taking and creating" mode — that's the time on the planet to do it
   - Act 3 (30 years): if you can take economics off the table, **find ways to give** — content, volunteering, mission-based company
   - *"If you can do that for 30 years, it will be more fulfilling than Act 1 and Act 2 combined — and it's tremendous for society."*
   - Lenny example: giving through passion while making a livelihood — *"A very powerful blend that is hard to achieve in Acts 1 and 2 given constraints. That's the liberation that equity provides."*

**Nikhail's own Act 3:**
- Devoting it to **coaching, giving, and lifting others** — but as a product person, the challenge is **scaling it authentically**
- *"It's a product problem. That's what I'll devote 30 years to."*

**Key advice:**
- *"It's never too early to start thinking through it."*
- Start working on your **next North Star before you catch the current rabbit**
- *"When you hit your skip, keep looking for the next skip."*

- Reason for that filter: the problems at that stage are most similar to each other

## 🧠 The Full Career Arc Framework

### Act 1 — Learning & Building
- Focus: craft, product, distribution, finding PMF
- Mode: taking and creating — this is the right time to be in that mode
- Key risk: short-term thinking, logo chasing, conflating promotion with career
- Key goal: pick a lane, build a story, think two jobs ahead

### Act 2 — Scaling & Leading
- Focus: building the factory that makes the car; organizational ambiguity; managing managers
- Mode: executing at scale, managing complexity, navigating politics
- Key risk: shadows of superpowers emerge; development areas hide behind strengths; ex-growth company traps
- Key goal: get real feedback, earn the right to manage, build the meeting OS, recognize your shadow

### Act 3 — Giving & Reinventing
- Focus: what comes after catching the rabbit
- Mode: giving — content, coaching, mission-based work, community building
- Key risk: becoming lost after success; creating war when peace is needed; falling into bad habits
- Key goal: build the next North Star before you need it; find ways to give at scale

## 💡 Tactical Advice Summary by Career Stage

### Early Career PMs
- Think **two jobs ahead** — work backwards from your end state
- Don't conflate **promotion with career advancement**
- Avoid **lateral moves** disguised as progress

- Seek **diverse experiences** across PMF stages and problem types — not logos

### Mid-Career PMs / New Managers

  1. No advocate
  2. Role doesn't exist
  3. Impatience
  4. Real development area you're not addressing

- **Credit people publicly** for giving you feedback → breaks down the barrier

- **Earn the right to manage** — wait to be invited in, then pick one specific area
- Check if you're at an **ex-growth company** — use the two diagnostic questions

### Senior Leaders / CPOs
- Actively look for your **shadows of superpowers** — go back through the discard pile of feedback

- Find a **trusted peer** who will give you the feedback for the right reasons with the right language
- Join or build a **community** of peers at the same stage — it's where real learning happens
- Start building your **Act 3 North Star** before you catch the current rabbit
- If economics are off the table: **orient Act 3 around giving** — it will be more fulfilling than Acts 1 and 2 combined

## 🏘️ Community as the New L&D

Nikhail's strong conviction: **community is the answer** to the manager training gap

| Mode | Quality |
|---|---|
| Self-service tools | Getting better |
| Structured teaching (L&D) | Weak — industry has massively underinvested |
| **Community** | **The answer** — safety, authenticity, shared experience, best practice exchange |

What community provides:
- You're **not alone** — others are going through the same thing
- **Real solutions** — someone in the group has already solved your problem
- **Safety** — can be authentic in ways you can't with co-workers
- **Accelerated learning** — faster than managing one random person on a project
