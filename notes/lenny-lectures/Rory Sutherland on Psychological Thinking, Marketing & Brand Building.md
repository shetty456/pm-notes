## 🧠 The Core Philosophy: Think Psychologically, Not Logically

### The Fundamental Problem with Rational Thinking
> *"We are trying to run the world of business entirely to a reductionist kind of maths and physics and finance model where everything's linear and everything works in straight lines."*

- Real life decisions are NOT like high school math problems
- High school math: single right answer, all information in the question is relevant, proportionality assumed
- Real life: multiple right answers, extraneous information, asymmetric information, imperfect trust
- *"We select and promote people on their ability to perform those artificial activities"* — a fundamental mismatch

### The Ship Captain Experiment
- French philosopher gave students a problem: ship stops, 27 goats get on, 3 sheep get off, 25 cows added — how old is the captain?
- Intelligent kids would not answer "impossible to tell" — they assumed all information in the question must be useful
- *"Are they so habituated by high school questions to assume that what's in the question must give you a single right answer?"*
- This is exactly how businesses make decisions — and it's wrong

### The Six Human Qualities (John Ralston Saul's Framework)
- From *Voltaire's Bastards* — the French Enlightenment became fixated with **reason** to the exclusion of everything else
- The six human qualities: reason, ethics, memory, instinct, creativity, and others
- *"Reason is not designed to be used in isolation"*
- Result of over-relying on reason: **Soviet-style capitalism** — quarterly targets, deeply demotivating, dehumanizing

### Why Irrationality Is Rational
**The Hare and the Dog:**
- When a hare is chased by a dog, it enters a completely random movement pattern (called "Crazy Ivan" in submarine warfare)
- It doesn't know what it's going to do next — this is the point
- If it knew, it would reveal preparatory muscular movements the dog could read
- *"The ability to behave irrationally is at the meta level, highly rational"*

**The Game Theory Argument:**
- If you're completely predictable, you'll spend your life being pushed around by people who know exactly what you'll do
- Some degree of unpredictability is a survival mechanism
- Darwin recognized this — we have a "random number generator" in our makeup

## 🎭 The Two Default Modes of Human Decision-Making

### 1. Habit
- Do what I've done before
- An organism that had to learn everything from first principles would eat a lot of poisonous berries
- *"I've always eaten the yellow berries. I seem to be fine. Let's just stick to the yellow berries."*

### 2. Social Copying
- Do what everybody else seems to be doing
- AI research finding: the single thing that would persuade people to get heat pumps is if **three people on their street** had one
- Not environmental benefits, not financial savings — social proof
- *"To an economist, that drives them practically insane"*

### The Electric Car Adoption Problem
- Early adopters of anything electrical have learned a heuristic: **wait, because things get better and cheaper quickly**
- This has been proven true for computers, DVDs, cassette decks, fax machines, flat screen TVs
- *"Are people wrong not to make that decision? Well, no. What they're doing is learning a heuristic from past experience."*

## 🏷️ The Marketing Invisibility Problem

### Why Marketing Gets No Credit
> *"When products succeed, we forget the extent to which marketing was actually instrumental or decisive in their success."*

- We "concertina history" — compress it into: there was no internet, then Tim Berners-Lee invented the web, and everybody wanted it
- Reality: Rory spent early career in the late 1990s **persuading people to get dial-up internet**
- We never say "I bought that product because of the great marketing" — we say "I bought it because it was a great product"
- *"But we thought it was a great product because of the marketing"*

### Steve Jobs Was a Marketer, Not a Technologist
> *"Steve Jobs was not a technologist. He was a pitch man. He was a huckster. He was a brilliant salesman. He was a fantastic marketer."*

- The tech people at Apple didn't respect him: *"I don't get what Steve even does. He can't even code."*
- But he was brilliant at: product design, focusing on a limited number of products, extraordinary taste, presentations, selling things as a magician
- *"Making everybody believe that Apple was capable of magic — and therefore you didn't need the skepticism"*

### Historical Marketing Examples
- **Smallpox vaccination**: Edward Jenner's marketing coup was getting the British royal family to vaccinate their children
- **Electricity in the home**: advertising campaign that ran for ~30 years
- **Penicillin**: came up against a lot of hostility in early stages
- **Mobile phones**: driven by social pressure — you looked like an asshole if you couldn't give someone your mobile number

## 🌀 The MAYA Principle: Maximally Advanced Yet Acceptable

### The Core Concept
- From designer Raymond Loewy
- There is a pace of change consumers will accept
- Consumers are more comfortable with **evolution** than complete reinvention
- *"Consumers like to migrate their behavior rather than reinventing their behavior"*

### The iPhone Example
- Even the iPhone was preceded by the iPod — it wasn't a complete WTF moment
- Consumers had already migrated to carrying a music device

### The Scandinavian Noir Example
- BBC 4 bought *The Killing* from Danish broadcaster for £24,000 (~$30,000)
- It became hugely popular → migrated to BBC 2 → Netflix bought the rights
- Why did it work? *"If you're British, the great thing about Scandinavians is they're just the right amount of weird."*
- Not so foreign you can't follow it, but weird enough to be interesting
- *"They're the right amount of weird — and I think that's true of product design"*

## 🔑 Idiosyncrasies Count Double

### The Jaguar Light Switch Story
- In 1990s Jaguars: the reading light was turned on with a switch on the **central console** (not by reaching up)
- Creative director at Ogilvy said: *"Keep those things. Distinctiveness really matters."*
- Jaguar was planning to get rid of it — thought it was inconsistent
- *"Actually distinctiveness really matters"*

### The Rolls-Royce Pedal
- For years, Rolls-Royces were the only cars with a **floor pedal** to dip headlamps (instead of a stalk)
- Completely unnecessary in modern cars — but distinctively Rolls-Royce

### The Veuve Clicquot Yellow Label
- Yellow labels ended up on the champagne by **mistake** (printing error)
- Sent to England to get rid of them — Brits loved it
- *"Can you send us more of the champagne with the yellow label?"*
- Now the entire identity of the brand: *"It's the one with the yellow label"*
- Visual distinctiveness created by accident → celebrated and amplified

### The DoubleTree Cookie
- Giving a warm cookie at check-in
- *"Extraordinary in terms of the attention it garnered"*
- A tiny idiosyncrasy that creates massive brand differentiation

### The MCI Friends & Family Plan
- Nominated specific numbers for 20-25% discount
- Most people make 80% of calls to 5-6 numbers (Pareto principle)
- Garnered far more interest than simply reducing all calls by 15-20%
- *"Things that are slightly weird, slightly counterintuitive — sometimes the right thing to do is celebrate them"*
- Generated comedy routines: *"I suddenly realized I couldn't think of the ninth number"*

### The Razor That Was Too Quiet
- Engineer used machine learning to make an electric razor insanely quiet
- Rory's warning: *"If you make it too quiet, we genuinely won't believe it's working"*
- The crackly noise and buzz **convinces us it's doing a job**
- *"You can over-optimize for things"*

## 💡 Great Products That Failed Due to Marketing/Timing

### Meta Portal TV
- *"One of the best things I've ever owned"* — bought four of them
- $500 worth of equipment selling for $120
- Every review said it was brilliant — but first seven paragraphs were about privacy paranoia
- *"If any product deserved to succeed in the pandemic, it was video conferencing on your TV"*
- Lesson: **a great product can be killed by the wrong narrative**

### Google Glass
- Launched too soon
- Only given to developers — *"developers probably aren't the world's coolest people"*
- User imagery was terrible — not the people you want walking around your bar wearing them
- Rory would have bought it at $1,000: *"I'd really like being able to walk around knowing what time it is, when my next meeting starts, what the weather's like"*

### The Wine Box
- Keeps for weeks, has its own tap, you can have one glass without opening a bottle
- *"Vastly better keeping in the fridge"*
- Didn't succeed — *"entirely psychological"*
- Logically superior product, psychologically wrong

### The Japanese Toilet
- *"I think it's barbaric that the Western Hemisphere dry wipes"*
- Cleans with water — objectively superior
- Failed to cross the chasm in Western markets
- *"The most expensive flat in London going for $110 million and you're going to wipe your own ass"*

### The Early Electric Car (Ford/Edison Era)
- Actually quite good: quiet, great acceleration, decent range
- Killed by **user imagery**: became stereotyped as a women's car
- Farm boys and racing enthusiasts all used gasoline cars
- Fast-forward to today: electric cars face the same user imagery problem (smug environmentalists)
- *"In very few cases are electric car owners actually very interested in the environment"*
- The people buying EVs in Essex and New Jersey are car enthusiasts and tech lovers — not environmentalists

## 🏢 Companies That Think Psychologically

### Octopus Energy / Kraken
- Multicellular structure: lots of small autonomous teams
- Ultimate objective is very clear, but teams of 10-15-20 have considerable autonomy in how they achieve it
- Extraordinary tariffs that marry supply and demand (charge car after midnight for a fraction of the cost)

### Shopify Customer Service Teams
- Toby Shannon (COO) modeled on sports teams: groups of 10
- Sports teams: rugby (15), soccer (11), cricket (11) — double-digit team sizes
- *"There was something meaningful about that"*
- Result: extraordinarily happy, motivated employees who kept each other motivated
- Small enough that pulling a sick day means your 8-9 teammates work significantly harder
- Natural human instincts of reciprocation and obligation kick in
- *"These instincts don't really scale up"* — the Dunbar number (150) is the limit

### AO (Appliances Online)
- Brief to staff: *"Treat the customer like you treat your grandmother"*
- The "shave test": would your mum be proud of what you've done today?
- Not metrics of speed and efficiency — a human brief that everyone understands
- *"Everybody knows what that means"*

### Zappos
- Refused to make speed a measure in the call center
- *"The call is as long as it needs to be to solve the problem"*
- Had a customer service call that was **seven hours long** — treated as a point of principle
- *"If it takes seven hours to solve the problem, that's how long we spend on the line"*

## 📊 The Metrics Problem: Soviet-Style Capitalism

### What Happens When You Over-Quantify
> *"We've eventually created what I call Soviet-style capitalism. It's deeply demotivating. It's all about quarterly targets."*

- *"At least the Soviet Union had a five-year plan. We've got these stupid quarterly obsessions."*
- Persistent cost-cutting has destroyed much of the pleasure of the workplace
- No discretionary judgment allowed — any deviation from the imaginary optimum is seen as a cost
- *"We go into work to some extent to exercise our humanity"* — which includes ethics, fairness, not just logic

### The Two Problems with Metrics
1. People **game them** to their own advantage
2. People **obey them** but find them stupid — loss of autonomy and judgment is deeply demotivating

### The Call Center Example
- Incentivized to get the customer off the phone as quickly as possible
- *"The loss of autonomy and judgment, the tokens — that is deeply depressing. It's deeply demotivating."*

## 🎯 The Walkman Lesson: Less Is More

### The Story
- Sony Walkman was built on the Sony Talkman (a dictating machine with speaker and microphone)
- Adding recording functionality would have cost **50 cents per unit**
- Engineers wanted to add it — doubles functionality for almost nothing
- Akio Morita (Sony) said **no**

### Why No Was Right
> *"You don't want to double the functionality of the device. You want the device to have one function, which it performs very well."*

- Once it has more than one function, people don't know where to start
- Is it a Dictaphone? Should I use it to record concerts?
- Ambiguity kills adoption
- *"Focus attention on one thing. If you want to do that thing, this is the thing for you. Very simple binary decision."*

### The McDonald's Parallel
- McDonald's: *"Do you want a Big Mac? Don't you want a Big Mac?"*
- American Diner before McDonald's: "How do you want your eggs? Over easy, sunny side up, poached, scrambled?"
- Choice reduction is sometimes the right answer
- Tesla's choice architecture: 5 colors, 2 interiors, 2 wheel sizes — *"just the right amount of choice"*

## 🔄 The Two-Stage Decision Making Process

### The Ancient Persian Method (Herodotus)
- Debated everything **twice**: once while sober, once while drunk
- Only if they agreed in both states would they proceed
- *"Does this appeal to us rationally? Does this also appeal to us emotionally?"*

### The Fundamental Asymmetry in Business
- Creative people have to present their ideas to rational people for approval
- **Never happens the other way around**
- Engineers and accountants never say: *"I think the answer is 3.75, but before I present this, I'm going to share it with some wacky people to see if they can come up with a neater idea"*

### Rory's Recommendation
- Share the logical, rational, typical answer first
- Then create dedicated time to think about the silly, crazy idea
- *"The opposite of a good idea can be another good idea"*

### The Hotel Check-In Example
- Two great ways to check into a hotel:
  1. **Insanely high touch**: Mandarin Oriental — taken to your room, shown how to use everything, tea made while you fill out paperwork
  2. **No touch**: walk in with mobile phone, unlock room yourself
- *"They're complete opposites, but they are both distinctive"*
- The problem is trying to find the "optimal" middle ground — which is neither

## 🌟 Brand Building for Startups: The Three Rules

### Rule 1: Be Consistent
- Stick at it visually without changing too much
- Brand building is like a pension — painful at first, then compounds dramatically
- *"For the first few years I had a pension: I'm paying this guy a few hundred quid a month and it's hardly gone up at all. I'm 58 now and I've got a pension and I go, 'Where did all this money come from?'"*

### Rule 2: Be Distinctive
- Idiosyncrasies count double
- Don't go crazy weird — MAYA principle applies
- But don't sand off all the interesting edges either

### Rule 3: Be Famous
> *"When you are not famous, you have to find all your customers. Suddenly you reach this magical escape velocity of fame where people start coming to you."*

**Why fame changes everything (non-linearly):**
- People call you back when you ring them
- People work for you for less money
- People apply to you without you having to find them
- People stay longer
- Customers give you the benefit of the doubt
- You're allowed to cock up once
- *"Having a great brand means you get to play the game of capitalism on easy mode"* — Eric Johnson

**The B2B Fame Principle:**
- *"No one ever got fired for buying IBM"*
- If you appoint PricewaterhouseCoopers and something goes wrong → they get blamed
- If you appoint someone nobody's heard of and something goes wrong → **you** get blamed for not appointing PwC
- The Ryanair vs. BA example: if BA is delayed, client says "never mind, you did your best"; if Ryanair is delayed, it reflects on your judgment

**Fame is non-attributable:**
- You can't say which activity built the brand
- Consumers don't know how they heard about you — they just did
- *"It's not even an amalgam of things. It's actually a concatenation — catalytic, not linear"*

### The Wrong Math Problem
- Everyone uses addition, multiplication, subtraction, division to quantify marketing value
- But marketing value is about **power laws and non-linearities**
- *"We are being judged by the wrong maths and evaluated over the wrong timeframe"*
- Rough estimate: marketing activity is probably **4x as valuable** as people think when measured by short-term contribution

## 🔬 The Survivorship Bias Problem

### We Only See the Successes
- We look at iPhone, Ford Model T and think: *"There was no smartphone, then Steve Jobs came along and everybody immediately wanted it"*
- We forget the 20 years of marketing, timing, luck, and failed attempts
- *"There are great products which are genuinely intrinsically a good idea that failed"*

### The Timing Problem
- 1989: Rory borrowed a mobile phone from the office (they had 8 to share), walked down Oxford Street, got shouted at from passing cars for using it
- Early 2000s: mentioned ordering groceries online — *"They laughed in my face"*
- Mid-90s: making an international call on a mobile phone on a bus — *"I actually thought there was a mild risk of physical attack"*
- *"A lot of younger people don't have the chronological context. They just assume there was no Internet, and then the Internet came along, so everybody got it."*

### The Definition of Insanity Reframe
> *"The definition of insanity is trying the same thing again and again and expecting a different result? That's not the definition of insanity. That's the definition of a complex system."*

- Quite a lot of internet ideas failed because they were mistimed
- Don't reject something because it failed in the past — the context may have changed

## 🎪 The Stand-Up Comedian Test

> *"If you could imagine a stand-up comedian doing a routine about your product, then you're onto something. The urge to appear serious is in many ways a disaster in marketing."*

### What This Means
- Comedians notice things that are slightly weird
- The MCI Friends & Family plan generated actual comedy routines: *"I suddenly realized I couldn't think of the ninth number"*
- These "splinters on the attention" — things that slightly raise up from the normal — have value
- *"The step that isn't quite the riser"* — if one step in a flight of stairs is slightly off, you notice it

### The Salman Rushdie Quote
> *"If you can get people to laugh, you can tell them anything."*

## 🦚 Why Rich People and Early Adopters Matter

### The Peacock's Tail Theory
- *"The first cars weren't as good as horses and carts. They were done to show off."*
- Birds evolved wings as sexual display plumage **before** they became large enough to function as propulsion
- Early adopters provide the early-stage funding for promising ideas before they reach maturity
- *"What rich people are for: they provide the early stage funding for promising ideas before they really reach maturity"*

### The Problem with Weird Early Adopters
- *"Weird people do not confer the reassurance of social norms"*
- If your weird millionaire has a wind farm and heat pump, it doesn't make ordinary people think "I must do the same"
- Air fryer example: Rory was an air fryer evangelist 15 years ago — *"I was like the John the Baptist of air fryers"*
- But evangelism can backfire: *"These people are in a cult"*

### The Marketing Department Blind Spot
- People in marketing are very high in **openness to experience** — keen to stand out, be distinctive
- The majority of the population is much lower on openness to experience
- *"You've got to be very careful working in a marketing department to continually remind yourself that you're an outlier"*

## 🔧 The Final Framework: Three Things in Parallel

### The Sweet Spot
> *"Try and do the psychological and the technological and the economic in parallel."*

1. **Works psychologically** — people want it, it fits human behavior
2. **Works technologically** — clever application of tech enables something new
3. **Works economically** — you can make money out of it

### Why Series Doesn't Work
- Businesses borrow from the Fordist/Taylorist production line mentality
- *"The process of any kind of innovation or development is not linear"*
- Many products failed at first iteration, then someone sold them differently and they succeeded

### The Wrigley's Pivot
- Started selling soap powder in Chicago
- Gave away baking powder free to sell soap powder
- Realized baking powder was more interesting → started selling that
- Gave away chewing gum as a gift with baking powder
- People liked the chewing gum more than the baking powder
- **Wrigley became a chewing gum company**

### The Two Paths to Innovation
> *"You can either work out what people want and find a clever way to make it, or you can work out what you can make and find a clever way to make people want it. The greatest things manage to do both."*

- Don't leave marketing to the last minute
- Work in parallel as far as you can
- *"Marketing and innovation are two sides of the same coin"*
