## 🔥 The Airbnb Intervention Story

### What Happened
- Joined Airbnb to take over the Experience Design Organization (10 people, previously reporting to a founder)
- Came in with knowledge of what wasn't working and "came in swinging, ready to go"
- One month in: got a calendar invite — Thursday 8:30 AM, 90 minutes, with 5 designers and an HR partner
- Walked into a white-walled room where five designers took turns reading from prepared papers listing everything she was doing wrong

### The Core Problem
> *"What was very clear was that the missing piece, the theme that was across all of that, is that I hadn't earned their trust."*

- Wasn't bringing the team along with her
- They had no idea they could trust what she was trying to build
- They didn't know she cared about them or had their best interests at heart

### The Turnaround
- Listened without responding defensively
- Immediately shifted how she was operating
- Started listening to what individuals on the team cared about and what motivated them
- Moved in the same direction but brought people along
- Result: A couple months later, **best engagement scores in the company**

### The Key Lesson
> *"You can inflict change on people, but if you want to do it with them, trust is the key element there."*

- Come in listening, not swinging
- Make change that has true positive impact on the folks around you

## 💰 The ROI of Design: Making the Case

### The Levels of Quality Framework
1. **Level 1**: Does the thing work? Does it provide the value proposition?
2. **Level 2**: Is it error-free and well-rounded?
3. **Level 3-5**: Does it exceed expectations? Does it do something users weren't even seeking?

### The Gym Analogy
> *"Every time I think to do this, there's a fight in my head of like, 'Ah, do I really need to work out today? Is this one day going to give me six-pack abs? Of course not. So why go?' But of course then at some point, hopefully, I realize that if I skip it today, what's to stop me from skipping it another day?"*

- Quality improvements add up over time even when individual improvements seem small
- The best companies believe in this compounding effect

### The Car Analogy
- First car: wheel was hard to turn, seat uncomfortable, only one color — but no competition (the competition was a horse)
- Today's cars: the stitching, the leather choice, the sound of the door — these distinguish okay cars from high-end ones
- Quality details matter when there's competition

### The Stripe Checkout Data
> *"99% of the top e-commerce sites have errors in their checkout flow that actually hinder more impactful, more seamless, quicker checkout, and therefore higher conversion."*

- Stripe powers checkout for Amazon, Hertz, Shopify, Spotify, X, and millions more
- By improving checkout quality through details small and large: **10.5% increase in business revenue** from older to newer checkout form
- Onboarding improvements → activation increases

### The Core Argument
> *"Quality is growth."*

- Making products easier to use and more understandable drives people to use it more
- Better experiences lead to word-of-mouth
- Beauty increases trust — seeing painstaking detail assures users you care about details they can't see too

## 🌟 What Is Beauty? What Is Great Design?

### The Declining Use of "Beauty"
- Stefan Sagmeister and Jessica Walsh's book *Beauty*: from the 1800s to 2000s, use of the word "beauty" in Google-digitized books has decreased dramatically
- This reflects the false belief that functionality and beauty are opposites

### Why Beauty Matters in B2B
1. **Increases trust**: Painstaking detail signals you care about details users can't see
2. **Easier to use**: Beauty enhances functionality, makes things more approachable
3. **Better user outcomes**: Equips users to make better decisions
4. **Beauty begets beauty**: When users see the care and creativity, it reassures them of your overall care
5. **Attracts talent**: People want their craft recognized and utilized

### The Penn Station vs. Grand Central Example
- Tweets from Penn Station: more negative
- Tweets from Grand Central: more positive and optimistic
- The environment you create has real impact on how people feel

### The Bear Reference
- "Peeling mushrooms" — the care put into details that nobody asked for but that elevate the whole experience

## 🏗️ Operationalizing Quality: The Essential Journeys Program

### The Problem It Solves
- Products can be at their highest quality when shipped, but quality regresses over time
- Teams get so focused on their area they forget how their piece connects to the whole
- Like renovating one room in a house — it makes everything else look worse

### The Five Principles of Building Quality (From Research with Other Leaders)

**1. Quality is a group effort**
- Can't hire one talented person and expect them to solve it
- QA alone won't solve it
- The way you run internal functions shows up externally
- Keep talent bar high AND share care across the organization

**2. Vision and alignment**
- Even the best people with great taste will end up with things that don't fit together without shared vision
- Like building a house: you need plans, a GC, an architect — even though a house is far less complex than most tech products

**3. Editing**
- Someone needs to see how all pieces fit together and help narrow, reduce, remove what doesn't fit
- Brian Chesky is the editor at Airbnb; The Economist has a chief editor
- Decentralizing this is possible but challenging

**4. Courage**
- The ability to say "No, this isn't good enough"
- "Almost, but no" is one of the hardest decisions leaders make
- A team puts all their care into something and you say "Let's try again"

**5. Journey thinking**
- Users rarely deal with any aspect of your product in isolation
- Must understand the experience from the user's point of view across the full journey

### The Essential Journeys Program (Stripe)

**Setup:**
- 15 most important user journeys (somewhat arbitrary but manageable)
- Each journey has engineering, product, and design leaders responsible for quality
- Leaders "walk the store" — review journeys as if walking the floor of their store

**The Process:**
1. Leaders go through the experience as a user would (starting from Google search, to website, to docs, to dashboard)
2. Friction log: screenshot + what they experienced, tagged by severity
3. File bugs, reach out to teams that own different parts
4. Score the journey using a rubric (usability, utility, desirability, surprisingly great)
5. Color-coded scoring system (not numbers) to avoid over-indexing on precision

**The Calibration Meeting (PQR — Product Quality Review):**
- Leaders present their scorecard and what they experienced
- Debate the scores: "Actually, that felt worse than you described" or "That was pretty great"
- Attendees: Katie, David Singleton (CTO), Will Gaybrick (product/business), relevant leaders
- Multidisciplinary room: product marketing, engineering, product all represented

**Cadence:** Quarterly for formal scoring; hope is teams do it weekly informally

**Unexpected Benefits:**
- Teams discovering SEO issues they hadn't noticed
- Engineers becoming converts who care about the full experience
- Leaders seeing their product as users do for the first time

## 🏃 The "Walk the Store" Practice

### What It Is
- Katie and David Singleton do this regularly outside the formal program
- Pick random flows and go through them together
- David codes, Katie observes: "What? Do they really do that? How can we make that better?"

### Why It Works
> *"Feeling the pain firsthand is just this next level of visceral understanding of like, 'Oh, this could be better.' Your users might not always say what's missing or what's wrong, or maybe they don't know that certain aspects of it could be better."*

- Doesn't replace user research — adds to it
- Users don't always articulate what's missing; firsthand experience reveals it

## 📊 The Design Deck / Show-and-Tell Practice

### What It Is
- Designers add screenshots or prototypes of what they're working on to a shared Google Slides deck
- Shared across the design team and with product managers, engineering leaders, and company leaders
- Currently monthly (was biweekly)

### Why It Works
- Designers see what's happening across the whole team
- Enables: "Oh, you're working on that surface, so am I — let's talk"
- Helps leaders understand what's being built and how pieces fit together
- Surfaces redundancy early: "This is the same project we're doing over here"
- Low maintenance: Google Slides is accessible to all functions, not just Figma users

### Key Principle
> *"A picture tells a thousand words and a prototype saves a thousand meetings."*

## ➗ The Performance Formula

### The Formula
**Performance = Potential − Interference**

### What This Means for Leaders
- **Increase potential**: Hire well, develop talent, help people grow
- **Decrease interference**: Remove the lead weight on top of great talent

> *"Like a muscle atrophying underneath a cast, if there are interferences that are holding them back from doing great work, they're going to burn out, they're not going to enjoy the work, they're not going to be as successful."*

### The Lyft Example
- Design team sat in a beautifully designed room separated from engineering and product by a locked door
- Benefits: creative space, wall-to-wall whiteboards, safe space for exploration
- Problem: Product managers and engineers were making decisions without designers; designers were working on misaligned things; wasted work, slower iteration
- Solution: Broke down the wall (literally and figuratively), brought teams together physically, kept creative space for working sessions

## 🏢 Org Design: How to Structure Design Teams

### The Two T-Shirts Model (from Joe Gebbia at Airbnb)
> *"You have two T-shirts. You have the Design T-shirt and you have the Marketplace T-shirt. Because both are really important communities to build for slightly different reasons."*

- Designers sit with their cross-functional teams (engineers, PMs) for day-to-day work
- Design team comes together at key moments to build community and align on shared goals

### The Embedded Model
- Designers assigned to specific product areas (driver, rider, safety, etc.)
- Sit with their respective engineers and product managers
- Come together as a design function to align on overall experience

### The Website Team Exception at Stripe
- Engineering and design both report into the design organization for the website
- Literally sitting side by side, batting ideas back and forth
- Engineer has great idea for execution; designer pushes it further
- Rapid iteration cycle at extremely high standard

## 🎯 Hiring Designers: What to Look For

### The Core Principle
> *"It's easier to teach tools and process than it is taste and character."*

### Three Key Attributes

**1. Taste and Judgment**
- Natural inclination for great things
- Hit rate for great judgment, even if not very experienced
- Context-dependent: dense information tools feel different than sparse consumer products

**2. Humility**
- Respect and empathy for colleagues and users
- Curiosity about what's working and what's not
- Willingness to pay attention to what users are saying

**3. Hustle / Chutzpah**
- Courage to take a blank piece of paper and propose something better
- Courage to say "This is not good enough and we should do it again"
- Rapid execution on that courage

### Junior vs. Senior Hire for Early-Stage Companies
- Early stages: probably need a doer
- But also need the lens of how to build a user-focused organization
- Recommendation: **Senior design advisor + junior executor/doer full-time**

## 🌟 Vision Work: Reach for the Stars, Land on the Moon

### The Problem with Incremental-Only Thinking
- Fear of bold ideas leads to incremental approaches
- Incremental is measurable and feels safer
- But if you only think about the scope of what you own, you're unlikely to make the whole thing better

### The Right Approach
> *"Reach for the stars and land on the moon."*

- Vision work that looks at the entirety of the experience
- Sketch out the ideal version (like Brian Chesky's 11-star experience)
- Then work back: "If this is what we want to get to in two years, how do we get there?"
- Ship pieces sequentially, study and learn, then move to the next piece
- The North Star makes progress feel like progress as you step toward it

### The House Analogy
- You want to see what the picture looks like in ideal form
- You can always work back from that
- Various parts of the organization own various parts
- Maybe ship this piece first to study it before moving to the next

## 📈 Performance Management and Quality

### The False Binary
- "Business goals vs. design goals" is a pet peeve
- The real question: "What are we trying to build towards?"
- Companies that want to create impactful products know quality serves the business long-term

### How to Bake Quality into Performance
1. **Redefine impact**: Not just "what business metric did I move?" — multi-quarter, multi-year work is still impactful
2. **Ladder documents**: Include quality expectations at each level; "meticulous craft" is an operating principle at Stripe
3. **Operating principles**: Meticulous craft expected of everyone — designers, engineers, support, everyone
4. **Show the examples**: Every company has examples where quality improvements moved business metrics; make these known

### The Support Case Example
- Users reaching out to support because they didn't know the state of their invoice
- Root cause: a button that looked nice but wasn't clear
- Fix: improved the button
- Result: decreased support contacts AND improved user experience
