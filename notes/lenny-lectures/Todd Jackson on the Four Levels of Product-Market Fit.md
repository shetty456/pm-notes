## 🎯 Why a Framework for PMF?

### The Problem with Existing Advice
> *"Product-market fit is mysterious to a lot of people. All the advice you find on the internet is very general — 'you'll know it when you see it.' It's not specific."*

- Most startup topics have good tactical content (hiring, board meetings, etc.)
- PMF content is almost entirely vague and non-actionable
- Rahul Vohra's Superhuman approach (published on First Round Review 2018) became famous precisely because it was specific and tactical

### Why PMF Is the Only Thing That Matters
> *"The only thing that matters in the first couple years is finding product-market fit. If you find extreme product-market fit, the momentum just carries you and the market pulls you along."*

- Easy to know what to build (customers tell you)
- Motivating for the team
- Easier to hire
- Everything becomes easier

### Who This Framework Is For
- **Early B2B founders** doing **sales-led** (not bottom-up/PLG) products
- First 6-12 months of starting the company
- Consumer products have more alchemy; B2B has more science

## 📊 The Three Dimensions of PMF

### The Ultimate Goal: Extreme PMF
> *"Extreme product-market fit is a state of widespread demand for a product that satisfies a critical need and crucially can be delivered repeatably and efficiently to each customer."*

**Three key dimensions:**
1. **Demand** — widespread desire for the product
2. **Satisfaction** — customers' critical needs are met
3. **Efficiency** — can be delivered repeatably and economically

### Why Efficiency Is Often Overlooked
- WeWork, Casper: achieved demand and satisfaction but never got efficiency right → businesses never worked at scale
- The $100 vending machine example: insane demand, great satisfaction, great retention — but completely unviable economically
- Many startups give away $2 for $1 and call it PMF

### The Marginal Customer Test
> *"If you have product-market fit, the next incremental customer you're going to get should be getting easier and easier to acquire and easier to serve."*

- As you progress through levels, the marginal customer gets easier
- This is the clearest signal of strengthening PMF

### The Key Insight: You Don't Optimize All Three at Once
- Different levels require different trade-offs
- Spending on marketing increases demand but decreases efficiency
- Investing in efficiency automation might harm customer experience
- **You make deliberate trade-offs at each level**

## 🔺 The Four Levels of Product-Market Fit

### Level 1: Nascent PMF

**What it looks like:**
- Pre-seed or seed stage
- Less than 10 people on the team
- $0 to $500K ARR
- Demand source: mostly people you know (friends, family, VCs, network)
- Takes ~20 warm intros to get 1 customer
- ~50 conversations to get 3-5 customers

**Primary focus: Satisfaction first, demand second, efficiency last**

**The job:** Find 3-5 customers who have an important, urgent problem and deliver a satisfying solution

**Key insight:** It's okay to be inefficient at this stage if it helps you deliver insanely good customer satisfaction

**Metrics NOT to worry about:** Burn multiple, gross margin, NRR — too early

**Signs you're stuck at Level 1:**
- Product disappeared overnight → customers wouldn't be super disappointed
- Have 4-5 customers but most important feature is different for each (looks like consulting, not a product)
- Incredibly hard to find the next customer
- Low usage despite having the product

### The Vanta Example (Level 1 Done Right)
- Christina Cacioppo tried B2B Alexa, dropshipping — didn't work
- Talked to security engineers, CISOs, CTOs: "What do you hate most about your job?"
- Consistent answer: filling out security questionnaires, compliance audits — "grungy manual work"
- First customers: Segment, Front, Figma — all trying to land enterprise deals
- Promise: "I'll get you SOC 2 compliance" → they landed the enterprise deal
- The product was literally Christina doing it manually — completely inefficient, but delivered the promise
- *"She was the one behind the email address posing as the AI, but doing it herself"*

### The Lattice Example (Pivot at Level 1)
- Started as OKR tool in 2015
- Customers would use it for one quarter, then stop
- Jack Altman kept the persona (HR leaders he'd gotten to know well) but changed the problem
- Timing: performance management was coming back in favor mid-2010s
- Showed Figma mock-ups of performance management tool — no product built yet
- Response was "off the charts" — sold first 5-10 customers with mock-ups
- *"Lattice kept the first one [persona] but changed the others"*

### The "Friend Zone" Test (Rick Song, Persona)
> *"I need your help. It is very important to me that this company succeeds. So I don't want you to be nice to me. I want you to tell me: is Persona a necessity for your company? If we went away, how painful would that be? If a competitor came along that charged half as much, would you switch?"*

### Level 2: Developing PMF

**What it looks like:**
- Seed or Series A stage
- Up to 20 people
- $500K to $5M ARR
- Sales conversion (first call to close): ~10%
- Starting to think about efficiency metrics (but not the focus)

**Primary focus: Demand (while maintaining satisfaction)**

**The job:** Go from 5 satisfied customers to 25 satisfied customers

**Key insight:** You can grind your way to 5-10 customers with willpower, but to get to 25, the product has to do heavy lifting

**Efficiency benchmarks (floor, not target):**
- Gross margin: not worse than 50%
- Burn multiple: not worse than 5x
- Regretted churn: not worse than 10-20%
- NRR: at least 100%
- Magic number: 0.5 to 0.75 range

**Signs you're stuck at Level 2:**
- Current customers are happy but you can't open the floodgates
- Regretted churn greater than 20%
- Sales cycles taking too long
- Losing deals late in the funnel
- Customers saying "we don't have budget" or "not the right time" (polite nos)
- Not starting to get known for a thing

> *"Most founders do a 10% pivot, and what they need to be doing is a 200% pivot."* — Jack Altman

### The Looker Example (Level 2 Done Right)
- Founded 2012 by Lloyd Tabb; business intelligence product
- Spent a long time at Level 1 but flew through Level 2
- Lloyd would go into customers and spend 20-30-40 hours before they were even a customer, modeling their data
- Called this their "forward deploy" process
- 75% close rate because they were only selling customers already using it
- Zero churn
- At 20 customers: "I know I'm onto something and I think I figured out a model"
- Also added SDRs, partner marketing with AWS Redshift, look-and-tell customer events in SF

### The Ironclad Example (Positioning Pivot at Level 2)
- Founded 2015 by Jason Boehmig; AI-powered contract management
- Originally called it "AI legal assistant" — hard to sell in 2014/2015
- Got a one-line email: "Are you a CLM?" (contract lifecycle management)
- Googled CLM, realized "we do that"
- Switched positioning from creating a new category to playing in an existing one (CLM) but better
- Won contract against 10-12 established vendors because product was more modern and automated
- *"When we changed our positioning to play in an existing category of CLM, but a much better CLM, things just started to click"*
- Ironclad website today still says: "AI-Powered Contract Management Software"

### The Plaid Example (Product Pivot)
- Started as a consumer budgeting app — not popular
- Had built the bank account connection infrastructure as part of the app
- Friends at Venmo, Robinhood, Coinbase wanted to license it
- Kept the product (the bank connection tech) but changed all three other Ps
- Went from: solving consumers' budgeting problems → solving developers' bank connection problems

### Level 3: Strong PMF

**What it looks like:**
- 30-100 people
- Series B territory (late Series A to early Series C)
- 100+ customers
- $5M to $25M ARR
- 10%+ of inbound from referrals and word of mouth
- At least one very scalable demand channel cracked

**Primary focus: All three — maintain satisfaction and demand, NOW add efficiency**

**The job:** Go from 25 customers to 100+ while getting efficient

**Efficiency benchmarks (now a real focus):**
- Gross margin: above 60%, ideally above 70%
- Burn multiple: 1-3x (ideally close to 1)
- Regretted churn: less than 10%
- NRR: greater than 110%

**This is when it starts to feel like PMF:**
> *"The boulder went down the other side and we had to chase it to keep up."* — David Hsu, Retool

> *"The biggest shift was in the ease of getting leads. I remember thinking, 'I don't even know where these leads are coming from, just more and more of them are showing up each month.'"* — Jack Altman, Lattice

> *"After our first year of sales in 2018, those next two years were crazy. We were barely keeping up with production."* — Filip Kaliszan, Verkada

**Signs you're stuck at Level 3:**
- Leaky bucket: NRR below 90%, regretted churn greater than 10%
- Growth slowing: grew 3x two years in a row, struggling to do 2x now
- First scalable channel getting saturated, need to find a new one
- Growing but spending too much to grow (burn multiple above 3)
- Increasing competition as others notice what you've built

---

### Level 4: Extreme PMF

**What it looks like:**
- 100+ people
- Series C, D or beyond
- 100+ customers heading toward 200, 300, eventually 1,000
- $25M+ ARR
- All efficiency metrics looking great

**Primary focus: Maintain all three + expand TAM**

**Efficiency benchmarks (now excellent):**
- Sales conversion first call to close: better than 15%
- Magic number: greater than 1
- CAC payback: less than 12 months
- Gross margin: above 80%
- Burn multiple: ideally less than 1
- Regretted churn: less than 10%
- NRR: greater than 120%

**The job:** Keep growing by expanding total addressable market

**How great companies expand TAM:**
- Bring product into new markets
- Build multiple products
- Examples:
  - Vanta: compliance → trust management platform → security questionnaires → vendor risk management
  - Verkada: security cameras → alarms → smoke detectors → badge readers
  - Stripe: core Stripe → Stripe Radar → Stripe Atlas
  - Square: Square Stand → Cash App → Square Checking → Square Loans

**The critical insight:**
> *"Just because you got to level four on your main product doesn't mean product-market fit is free on all these new products."*

- Finding PMF is never done — you have to find it again and again for each new product
- Customer expectations constantly rise (Casey Winters's point)
- Competition increases as you grow

## 🔄 The Four Ps: What to Change When You're Stuck

### The Framework
Every product must align four elements:
1. **Persona** — who you're targeting (think of the market as a collection of people, not a macro category)
2. **Problem** — what challenge they have that's important and urgent
3. **Promise** — what you're committing to deliver (your positioning)
4. **Product** — what you actually build to satisfy the other three

> *"Your product has to deliver a promise that solves the problem of your persona."*

### How Different Companies Used the Four Ps

| Company | What They Changed | What They Kept |
|---|---|---|
| **Lattice** | Problem, Promise, Product | Persona (HR leaders) |
| **Ironclad** | Promise (positioning) | Persona, Problem, Product |
| **Plaid** | Persona, Problem, Promise | Product (bank connection tech) |
| **Vanta** | All four | Nothing from original ideas |

### Which P to Change First
- Persona and problem are easier to change than rebuilding the product
- Start there before rebuilding
- The persona choice is the most important choice founders make
- Founders spend 99% of time building, 1% picking — but the pick determines everything

> *"Founders spend 99% of their time building because that's what they've done. And they spend 1% of their time picking the market, picking the problem, picking the customer. And in reality, it's that pick that determines the constraints and the boundaries of where you're going to be working for the next, hopefully, 10 years of your life."* — Josh Kopelman, First Round

## 💰 Dollar-Driven Customer Discovery

### The Problem with Normal Customer Discovery
- Customers are nice — they'll be polite
- They're not good at predicting their own behavior
- They're good at talking about problems but not at predicting what they'll buy

### The Goal: Identify Extreme Value
- Ask about their top 3 goals for the next 3 months (non-leading)
- Avoid "happy ears" — looking for things that support what you're already building
- Look for **wow statements**: "Does that really work? If that thing works, I'd sign up for the wait list today"
- Look for **demonstrated behavior**: "Can we meet again next week?" / "Can you send me the deck to share with my team?"
- The word "interesting" = a polite no

### Confirming Ability to Pay
Key questions:
- Are you currently looking for a product like this, or building something internally?
- Where would a budget for this come from? (Best answer: existing budget that can be displaced)
- How does your team make decisions on third-party tools?

### Confirming Willingness to Pay (Madhavan Ramanujam's Method)
Three questions in sequence:
1. "What is a **fair** price you would pay for this?"
2. "What would be an **expensive** price?"
3. "What would be a **prohibitively expensive** price?"

**The insight:** The "fair" price is what they hope to pay. The "expensive" price is what they'd actually pay if the product is good. The "prohibitively expensive" price is the ceiling.

### What Fidelity to Show
- **Lattice**: Figma mock-ups were enough (performance management is easy to visualize)
- **Looker**: Had to demo with real customer data (you don't get it until you see your own data)
- **Vanta**: Had to actually do the work (no mock-up or demo could substitute)

### When You've Talked to Enough People
> *"If you talk to enough people and you can predict 70-80% of what the next person is going to say to you, because you've heard the pattern so clearly — that's when you've talked to enough people."*

## 📊 PMF Timeline and Survival Rates

### Rough Timeline to Extreme PMF
- **Total**: ~4-6 years (pick 5 as the number)
- **Level 1**: 12-18 months (most important — choosing the right persona and problem)
- **Level 2**: ~1 year (if Level 1 was done well)
- **Level 3**: ~2 years ($5M to $25M ARR)
- **Level 4**: ~2 years ($25M to $100M+ ARR)

### Survival Rates
- **~60-70%** of companies get stuck at Level 1 or Level 2 and never progress
- **~30%** make it to Level 3 or Level 4
- First Round's goal: get that 30% number above 50%

### When to Consider Stopping
> *"If you've been going at it for four to five years and you haven't started to find anything that you're really feeling pull from the market on, what are the chances that you're going to magically find something?"*

- No shame in returning money to investors or finding a soft landing
- Product-market fit is very, very hard — that's why this framework exists
