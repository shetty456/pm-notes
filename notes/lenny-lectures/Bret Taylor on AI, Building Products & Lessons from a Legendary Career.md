## ❌ Fail Corner: The Google Local Story

### The Failure
- Joined Google in late 2002/early 2003 as one of the earliest associate product managers
- Given the opportunity to lead local search — a big bet from Marissa Mayer
- Built **Google Local**: essentially a digital version of Yahoo Yellow Pages
- Had a link on the Google homepage (massive traffic advantage) — and still underperformed
- *"To not do that well with a link from the Google homepage is embarrassing. There's not much one can do more than giving you that kind of traffic."*
- Had a tough product review with Marissa Mayer and Larry Page

### The Lesson
> *"Why use this instead of Yahoo Yellow Pages? But more than anything else, why use this instead of the Yellow Pages? It was a digital version of something that had come before."*

- The product worked but wasn't differentiated
- Digitizing what came before ≠ creating something compelling

### The Recovery: Google Maps
- Given another shot at V2
- Key insight: the map was always the ugly part of the product — what if they inverted the hierarchy and made the map the canvas?
- Found Lars and Jens Rasmussen (who had been working on a Windows mapping product)
- Integrated mapping, local search, and driving directions — all previously separate product categories
- **Launch result**: 10 million people using it on the first day
- **Satellite imagery integration** (from Keyhole/Google Earth acquisition): 90 million people on the same day
- Featured on Saturday Night Live (Andy Samberg's "Lazy Sunday")

### The Product Lessons from Google Maps
1. **Don't just digitize what came before** — create an entirely new experience native to the platform
2. **The "why" matters**: why should someone give this the time of day?
3. **Sizzle vs. steak**: satellite imagery wasn't the most important feature, but it created the viral moment
4. **Enduring value vs. initial hook**: these are deeply related but not the same thing

## 🧠 The Mindset Behind Success Across Many Roles

### The Core Principle: Flexible Identity
> *"I probably would self-describe as an engineer, but more broadly I think of myself as a builder. To really build something of significance, you really need to be able to not have such an ossified view of your identity that you can't transform into what the company needs you to be at that point."*

- Different colleagues think of him through different lenses (engineer at Facebook, product person at Google, "the boss" at Salesforce)
- Every founder needs to sell, design, engineer, and manage — you can't be a single-issue voter

### The Sheryl Sandberg Moment
- Became CTO of Facebook, then had a large reorganization where he suddenly managed 1,000+ people (platform and mobile groups)
- Sheryl caught him editing a presentation himself because it didn't meet his quality bar
- She gave him direct feedback about holding his team to high standards and managing people out if they weren't meeting expectations

**The realization:**
> *"I had this subconscious limiter that was limiting my success in the job, which is I was trying to conform the job to the things I thought I liked to do."*

- Was spending time on product and technology things he was passionate about instead of asking: "What's the most important thing to do today to make our mobile and developer platform successful?"

**The transformation:**
- Reframed the job around impact, not personal preference
- Discovered he liked things he thought he didn't like (partnerships, organizational change) when they produced results
- *"What I really liked is impact."*

### The Daily Practice
> *"Waking up every morning, what is the most impactful thing I could do today?"*

- Think like an external board of advisors: what are the things where if you focus on them, you maximize the likelihood of achieving your goal?
- Sometimes it's recruiting, sometimes product, sometimes engineering, sometimes sales
- The question is easy to ask; answering it accurately is the hard part

## ⚠️ The Danger of Incorrect Storytelling

### The Core Problem
> *"One of the dangers for founders and product managers, but particularly for founders, is incorrect storytelling. People don't like my product because of X. And if you tell that to yourself and you tell it to your team, all of a sudden it goes from being an intuition to being a fact."*

### The Break-Up Analogy
- When you break up with someone, you say "it's not you, it's me" — pleasantries because we're social animals
- Customers do the same: "It's too expensive" might actually mean "I didn't see enough value"
- You could end up fixing pricing when the real problem is product differentiation

### The Single-Issue Voter Problem
- Engineers think engineering is the answer to almost every problem
- Product designers think the next redesign will fix everything (*"the dead cap balance of a consumer product"*)
- Business development people think the right partnership will change everything

> *"If you think the thing that you've been doing your whole career is the way to fix your problem, it's at least 30% likely that you've chosen that because of comfort and familiarity, not truth."*

### The FriendFeed Lesson
- Had 12 of the best people he'd ever worked with
- Were innovating faster than Twitter, had more features, were up 100% of the time while Twitter had the fail whale
- Lost anyway — because Biz Stone was focused on getting celebrities (Obama, Ashton Kutcher, Oprah) onto Twitter
- *"We were exclusively focused on polishing the product... We totally lost for no reason related to product at all."*
- Lesson: weren't soliciting advice from the right people; were myopic in their own world

## 💡 How to Get Good Advice

### The Challenge
> *"There's not a strong correlation between the confidence with which someone expresses an opinion and the quality of that opinion."*

- The most eloquent, confident statements about things Bret knows a lot about are sometimes the least accurate

### Heuristics for Finding Good Advisors
1. **Ask who to talk to**: "Who should I talk to to get good advice?" — common answers signal good judgment
2. **Ask why, not just what**: Be an obnoxious two-year-old. Why? Why? Why?
   - People extrapolate from relatively few experiences and present them as rules
   - Understanding the framework behind advice lets you apply it with nuance
3. **Triangulate**: If three people give similar advice with similar underlying experiences, you can build a first-principles framework

### The Importance of Good Judgment
- Hire for it; it's one of the most important traits
- Comes from self-reflection: when you make a bad decision, spend time understanding why
- No one's advice is statistically significant (unless you're getting investing advice from Warren Buffett)

## 💻 The Future of Coding

### Should People Still Learn to Code?
> *"I do still think studying computer science is a different answer than learning to code, but I would say I still think it's extremely valuable to study computer science."*

- Computer science teaches **systems thinking** — understanding Big O notation, complexity theory, algorithms, cache misses
- The act of creating software is transforming from typing into a terminal to **operating a code-generating machine**
- Operating that machine requires systems thinking

### The Newsfeed Design Example
- Beautiful Photoshop mockup of the newsfeed looked perfect
- Implement it and it looks terrible because real content is messy
- *"Designing a newsfeed, Photoshop is the easy part. You need to actually design a system that produces a delightful experience given input you don't control."*
- This is systems thinking — and AI won't eliminate the need for it

### The Future of Programming Languages/Systems
> *"If the act of writing code is going from something that is very costly to the marginal cost of that going to zero, how many of the abstractions that we've built are based on human programmer productivity?"*

- Python is designed to be ergonomic for humans — but it's inefficient and hard to verify
- If humans aren't writing code, ergonomics don't matter — **verifiability** does
- **Rust example**: You can verify a Rust program doesn't leak memory just by compiling it — compile-time memory safety
- We need more things like that for AI-generated code

**The vision:**
- A "programming system" (not just a language) designed for AI-generated code
- Layers: code generation + code review AI + formal verification + unit testing + supervisor models
- Goal: operators of code-generating machines can produce incredibly complex, robust systems quickly and know they work
- *"VibeCoding is exciting, but generating a prototype has never been the limiting factor in software. Maintaining complex systems and changing them with agility is hard."*

## 🤖 The AI Market: Where to Build

### Three Segments

**1. Frontier/Foundation Model Market**
- Will end up as a small handful of hyperscalers and big labs — like cloud infrastructure
- Requires massive CapEx; startups that tried have mostly been consolidated (Inflection, Adept, Character)
- *"No entrepreneur should build a frontier model."* (Unless you're Elon)

**2. AI Tooling Market**
- Data labeling, data platforms, eval tools, specialized models (like ElevenLabs for voice)
- Risk: close to the sun — infrastructure providers keep moving up the stack
- Some meaningful companies (Snowflake, Databricks, Confluent) but many others were obviated
- *"There's probably a lot of people who need your tool, but the question will be if or when one of these large infrastructure providers introduces a competitor"*

**3. Applied AI / Agent Market**
> *"Agent is the new app. That's going to be the product form factor."*

- Companies like Sierra (customer service), Harvey (legal), content marketing, supply chain analysis
- Higher margin because you're selling business outcomes, not byproducts of models
- Will evolve like SaaS: less about technology, more about product over time
- *"Just as getting a database running in the cloud was a technical achievement in 1998 and is now trivial, orchestrating an agentic process on top of models will be easy in 3-4 years"*

### The Long Tail Opportunity
- Looking at top 50 software companies: top 5 are hyperscalers, next 50 are all SaaS
- Agents will follow the same pattern — not just huge markets like customer service and software engineering
- *"It's going to be a lot of things where people are spending a lot of time and resources that an agent can just solve, but it requires an entrepreneur who actually understands that business problem deeply"*

## 💰 Why Agents Are a Step Change

### The Productivity Paradox
- First wave of computing (ERP, accounting in computers) drove huge productivity gains
- Subsequent software waves haven't bent the curve as much as expected
- Selling productivity software is hard: *"If every salesperson sells 5% more... it's so unattributable"*

### Why Agents Are Different
1. **Autonomous job completion**: Software goes from helping individuals be slightly more productive to actually accomplishing a job autonomously
2. **Measurable outcomes**: Unlike "made everyone 10% more productive," you can actually measure if the agent solved the customer's problem
3. **Outcomes-based pricing**: Aligns vendor and customer incentives completely

> *"The whole market is going to go towards agents. I think the whole market is going to go towards outcomes-based pricing. It's just so obviously the correct way to build and sell software."*

### The Drafts People Analogy
- Mechanical engineering firms used to have majority drafts people (translating designs into contractor-ready drawings)
- AutoCAD/Revit eliminated drafting as a job — the design IS the output now
- Agents will do the same for many knowledge work tasks

## 💵 Outcomes-Based Pricing

### How It Works at Sierra
- Customer service agents handle calls/chats autonomously
- If the AI agent solves the customer's problem without human intervention = "resolution" or "containment"
- Pre-negotiated rate per resolution (call it ~$15 saved vs. human agent)
- Some sales agents paid a sales commission
- *"We want to make sure that our business model is aligned with our customer's business model"*

### Why It's Better Than Usage-Based Pricing
- Tokens ≠ value (like measuring engineer productivity by lines of code)
- Famous Apple engineer story: submitted a negative number of lines of code after a refactoring — his way of saying the metric was idiotic
- A long phone call that doesn't solve the problem adds negative value
- *"There's a huge difference between outcomes-based pricing and usage-based pricing because especially in AI, they're not necessarily even correlated"*

### Requirements for Outcomes-Based Pricing
1. The agent must be autonomous
2. The outcome must be measurable
- Not always possible, but broadly possible for many use cases

### The Strategic Benefit
> *"Every technology company aspires to be a partner, not a vendor. At Sierra, we are truly a partner to every single one of our customers because we're all aligned on what we want to achieve."*

## 📈 AI Productivity: What's Actually Working

### The Current State
- Most engineering firms use Cursor as coding autocomplete
- Problem: AI-generated code often has errors; reviewing someone else's code is harder than editing your own
- *"You could end up producing lots of features but actually mucking up the machine a little bit"*

### The Solution: Systems Thinking for AI
**1. AI supervising AI**
- If you have an agent right 90% of the time, build another agent to find the other 10%
- If that's right 90% of the time → wire together → 99% accuracy
- *"You can layer on more layers of cognition and thinking and reasoning and produce things increasingly robust"*

**2. Root Cause Analysis (Sierra's approach)**
- One engineer exclusively focuses on the MCP server serving their Cursor instance
- Philosophy: if Cursor generated something incorrect, don't just fix it — root cause it
- *"Try to get it so the next time Cursor will produce the correct code — essentially context engineering"*
- What context did Cursor not have that would've been necessary to produce the right outcome?

> *"People are waiting for the models to just magically get better. If you want the gains now, you got to put in the work."*

### Sierra's Results
- 50-90% of customer service interactions completely automated
- Customers: health insurance, healthcare providers, banks, telecom (DIRECTV, SiriusXM), retailers (Wayfair, OluKai, Chubbies)
- Weight Watchers agent: 4.6/5 CSAT score
- Airport agent: 4.7/5 CSAT score (people coming in with problems, leaving delighted)
- Even technical support for CAT scan machines

## 🚀 Go-to-Market for AI Products

### Three Proven Go-to-Market Models

**1. Developer-Led (Stripe, Twilio model)**
- Appeals to individual engineers with latitude to choose solutions
- Works for: platform products, selling to startups
- Doesn't work for: line-of-business products where buyers ≠ users

**2. Product-Led Growth**
- Users sign up from website, trial, buy with credit card
- Works when: user and buyer are the same person (small business software)
- Doesn't work when: buyer and user are different (expense reporting: employee uses it, finance buys it)

**3. Direct Sales (Oracle, SAP, Salesforce model)**
- Coming back into fashion with AI companies
- *"So many of the opportunities in AI actually meet that qualification where the buyer and the user are not necessarily the same person"*

### The Common Mistake
> *"Where I see entrepreneurs stumble is they'll choose a go-to-market motion without thinking through what is the process of purchasing this software? What is the process of evaluating the value of this software?"*

- Many founders avoid direct sales because of its reputation
- But many AI products require it — and it's coming back into fashion

## 👨‍👧 Teaching Kids in the Age of AI

### The Calculator Analogy
- When graphing calculators were allowed in AP Calculus exams, the exam had to change to test calculus knowledge that didn't benefit from calculators
- We haven't gone through that transition with ChatGPT yet — most educational evaluation mechanisms are broken

### What Bret Does with His Kids
- Makes his daughter use ChatGPT instead of asking him coding questions
- Daughter took a picture of a Shakespeare page she didn't understand — ChatGPT explained it better than he would have
- Kids use ChatGPT to quiz themselves before tests (better than cue cards)

> *"Every child in this world has a personalized tutor that can teach them in the way that they best learn."*

### The Nuance
- AI is different from social media/phones — it's more like Google search (a utility)
- The concern: *"These technologies amplify agency. The opposite can also be true — if you are a student trying to not learn something, these tools probably provide a lot of mechanisms to avoid it as well."*
- Hopeful: a 11-year-old right now whose primary tutor is ChatGPT will start an amazing company in 10 years

## 🔘 The Like Button Origin Story

### The Problem It Solved
- FriendFeed had discussions under posts, but 70% of comments were just "cool," "wow," "yeah," "neat"
- Goal: get one-word acknowledgements out so actual discussion could happen
- Original framing: **"one-click comment"**

### The Design Process
- First version had a **heart** icon
- Anna Yang (now Anna Muller) hated it: *"If I look at hearts on every post, I'm going to vomit. It's too much."*
- Also: a heart on an article about a tragedy felt wrong
- Needed something positive yet as neutral as possible within the realm of positive
- Landed on **"like"** — subtle enough to work for complex stories

> *"It was all because we needed a one-click comment. That's where the concept came from."*
