## 🌐 The Big Picture: How Big Is AI Really?

### The Controversial Take
> *"My most controversial opinion is that I think that AI is as big a deal as the internet or mobile — and only as big a deal as the internet or mobile."*

- Two camps disagree with this:
  1. Tech insiders who think it's bigger (industrial revolution scale)
  2. People who think he's overstating it
- His response: smartphones were quite a big deal. The internet was quite a big deal. We wouldn't be doing this podcast without the internet.

### The 1997 Analogy
> *"If you're going to make the internet comparison, it's like we're in 1997. It's very exciting. Most stuff kind of doesn't work yet. Most of the stuff that people are going to do hasn't been built yet."*

- The people who've "taken the pill" imagine everyone in the world is already there
- Reality: wide distribution of adoption
  - Tech people: bought Mac mini clusters, don't use Google anymore
  - Outside tech: most users use it every week or two, if at all
  - Even 13-18 year olds: only ~15-20% are daily active users; another 20% weekly; 60% not using it

### The Fractal Point
- Super high level: this is going to change absolutely everything
- But you don't know how any of it is going to work
- Comparing models (is it Excite or Yahoo?) is like being in 1997 — the answer was "neither"
- *"It's 80 slides saying we don't know"*

## 📊 The Jagged Frontier Problem

### The Accountant and the Spreadsheet Analogy
- Imagine being an accountant seeing the first software spreadsheets in the late '70s
- Mind-blowing: change the interest rate and all numbers update instantly, does a week of work in 30 seconds
- But if you're a lawyer or journalist looking at that: "That's very clever, my accountant should see this, but that's not what I do"
- You needed a word processor (which came shortly after)

**The current moment:**
- Software developers = the accountants seeing VisiCalc ("before Claude Code and after Claude Code")
- Many other people: picking it up, using it to varying degrees, but slightly puzzled

### Where It's Already Transformative
- Software development: unquestionably already in that moment
- Questions remain: what does agentic + AI software development mean for the software industry?
- One extreme: you'll just vibe code your own Stripe (no one really believes this)
- Other extreme: if you're in a law firm, how exactly do you use this? How do you avoid submitting something with hallucinations?

## 💼 The Professional Services Paradox

### Why AI Labs Are Investing in Consultancies
> *"You would think AI is going to make consultants gone. Instead, the most cutting edge AI labs are the ones most investing in these folks."*

**The reason:**
- Companies don't have people sitting around waiting to do big new projects
- To reimagine all internal workflows and work out which could be automated: that's a project
- That project needs 5-10 people to sit down for a month or two
- Then actually doing it is another project
- Who's going to do that? You don't have people sitting around not doing anything

**The joke:**
> *"A forward deployed engineer is like an Accenture outsourced software developer who lives in San Francisco."*

### What Are You Actually Paying McKinsey For?
- Not the 75-slide deck (Claude can make a crappy version of that)
- What you actually pay Bain to do: walk all over your enterprise, work out why you didn't do that, how the politics work, what you actually need to do, talk to your customers
- The PowerPoint is just the task — that's not what you hired them for

**The Amazon analogy:**
- Amazon gets you the SKU — if you know what you want, you can get it
- But knowing what SKU you want is another job
- Claude Code can write you the code — but what code do you want? What features? Who's your customer? What's the right product?

## 🤖 The Task vs. The Job

### The Core Framework
> *"What's the hard part of the job? Is the hard part writing the code line by line? Is the hard part making the PowerPoint? Or is the hard part something else? Is it the task or the job?"*

**The elevator attendant example:**
- Manual elevator: there's a lever, someone drives you to your floor
- Those got automated in the '50s — pressing the button became the job
- Some things: the job was a task and the task got automated

**The Jevons Paradox / Price Elasticity:**
- If you make it cheaper to do something, what happens?
- Do you do the same for less money?
- Do you do more for the same amount of money?
- Do you do more for more money because you've got new ROI?

**The accounting example:**
> *"Young people won't believe this, but before Excel, junior investment bankers worked really long hours. And now thanks to Excel, Goldman's associates all work at lunchtime on Fridays."*
- That's NOT what happened
- Adding machines → punch cards → mainframes → databases → ERP → cloud → spreadsheets → PCs
- Number of accountants kept going UP through all of this

**The software development example:**
- Before IDEs and libraries, developers had to write all the code
- Now if you write an iPhone app, 90% of the code is written for you by Apple
- "So we've got like a tenth as many engineers now." — No. We don't.

## 💼 The Jobs Question

### The Doomer Critique
> *"You talk to these doomers on Twitter and they would act like every big company is going to buy ChatGPT tomorrow and then in two weeks time they'll fire all their staff. These people are morons."*

**Why this is wrong:**
- Typical enterprise software sales cycle: 18 months if you're lucky
- No one is just going to tear out SAP and replace it with XYZ
- Maybe in 3-5-10 years that whole estate looks radically different
- It will take time sector by sector for people to work out "oh, you could do that with this"

**The Frame.io example:**
- Nothing there you couldn't have done 5-10 years earlier
- The delay was somebody realizing "oh, that problem exists inside that industry and this is how we'd solve it"
- It didn't all happen the day after Google Docs — took 10-15-20 years

### The Historical Pattern
> *"Every time we have a new technology, it automates away a bunch of jobs and then that automation unlocks a bunch of new jobs and you don't know the new job because it doesn't exist yet."*

- 1800: 90% of us were peasants worried about crop failure
- Ever since: automating jobs, creating new jobs
- You can always see the job going away; you don't know the new job because it sounds dumb anyway
- "Railway engineer — what's a railway? Why would that be a thing? Who would care who would want to go that fast?"
- Each time: frictional pain, dislocation, people lose jobs, towns get hollowed out — it all sucks
- But on the other side: we're all richer and not worried about crops failing

### Is This Time Different?
**The speed argument:**
- AI adoption is quicker than previous technologies — but that's because you're standing on the shoulders of giants
- ChatGPT can get 900 million users because there are already 900 million people on the internet
- When Netscape launched in '93-'94, there were only 50-100 million PCs on Earth
- The internet was faster than what came before too

**The argument from authority problem:**
> *"I'm interested in Dario's opinions on where models are going to go in the next 6-12 months. I'm not particularly interested in his opinions on theories of labor and market value and comparative advantage — yeah, maybe he had a course on that at university. So did I."*

### The Current Employment Data
- No clear consensus that we're seeing an impact on jobs yet
- Slowdown in employment of 18-24 year olds — but same for people with and without degrees, same for fields exposed and not exposed to AI
- We have very little data: model labs don't give us meaningful usage information, no daily active user numbers

## 🏗️ Where the Value Will Accrue

### The Three-Part Framework (from Benedict's presentation)

**1. Capital** — Where is all the CapEx going? Do model labs have differentiation?

**2. Deployment** — What does this mean for the software industry?

**3. Change** — How does this change stuff?

### The Foundation Model Question
> *"My dear sweet child, you need me to explain the margin structure of the utility industry to you."*

**The telecom analogy:**
- Global mobile industry: ~$1 trillion revenue/year
- Spends ~$200 billion/year on CapEx (15-20% of revenue)
- Mobile data consumption: 1,500-2,000x what it was in 2010
- **The stocks have gone nowhere in 25 years**
- It's an ex-growth, low-margin commodity utility

**The key question:**
- Do the models have network effects? (Doesn't seem like it — no winner-takes-all effect)
- If no network effects → competition indefinitely
- If competition indefinitely → no pricing power
- If no pricing power → value goes further up the stack

**The Windows vs. AWS comparison:**
- Windows: developers standardized on it because all customers used it; customers used it because all software was on it — real lock-in
- AWS: if you're an engineering company or law firm buying software, you don't care which cloud it runs on — no lock-in
- Foundation models look more like AWS than Windows

**The conclusion:**
> *"If the chatbot isn't the UX and it needs to be apps and the model companies aren't going to build that and the models themselves are basically commodities... why would the model companies have pricing power? Wouldn't all the value be further up the stack?"*

### The Distribution Moat
> *"If the product is a commodity, then the distribution is what matters."*

- Google using distribution to drive Gemini
- Meta: survey data showed it was up there between ChatGPT and Gemini — they'd "sprayed it on every service surface and it wasn't that bad"
- The power of the default: why would you switch?
- Apple: a billion devices that can run AI on edge
- Google: "coming soon to our most powerful devices" = won't work on most Androids

### The Recorded Music Analogy
- U-shaped revenue curve: dropped by half from 2000-2015, came back to ~75% of peak
- First half: what happens if I don't have to pay $15 for a CD to get that track?
- Second half: what happens if $15/month gives you all the music that exists?
- Spotify is not an online music store — it's something else entirely
- The pattern: first you do the old thing but more → then you make new things only possible with the new platform → then you completely redefine the question

## 🚫 The Anti-AI Sentiment

### What's Actually Going On
> *"It's a big fuzzy mess of different stuff."*

**The water thing:**
- Data centers use water for cooling (mostly closed loop)
- Livermore Lab study: US data center water consumption = ~0.017% of US water consumption
- "If you live in a small town and they capped the well and gave all the water to the data center, you're really pissed off — but that's a planning problem, not a data center problem"

**The energy thing:**
- Data centers: ~5% of US energy
- Might grow 1 percentage point per year for the next 5 years
- Real but manageable

**The jobs thing:**
- Lots of econometric argument, no clear consensus
- Slowdown in youth employment but not clearly AI-caused

**The culture war:**
- People who draw book covers for young adult romance novels upset about AI imagery
- "AI slop" question
- 30-40% of new podcasts generated by AI

**The comparison to social media backlash:**
- Some of the backlash around social was true, some was sort of true, some wasn't
- "Facebook sells your data" — not true, but people are absolutely adamant it is
- *"You can't reason somebody out of an idea they won't reasonably into"* — Jonathan Swift

### The Deep Fake Problem
> *"A 15-year-old kid couldn't use Photoshop to make hardcore pornographic nudes of every girl in their high school and send them to the whole school in one afternoon. Now they can."*

- The "haven't you heard of Photoshop?" response is technically true but misses the point
- Scale and accessibility change the nature of the harm

### The Post Office Scandal (UK)
- Post office rolled out a new Fujitsu point-of-sale system with bugs showing false cash shortfalls
- Post office concluded people were stealing → hundreds prosecuted, suicides, bankruptcies, people lost homes
- Fujitsu and post office employees swore in court there were no bugs
- *"Every wave of technology comes with ways that you can ruin people's lives either deliberately or by accident"*

## 🔮 AGI and Super Intelligence

### The Honest Answer
> *"We have no theory of what human intelligence is. We have no theory of why these models work so well. We have no theory of how much better they will get. So we're all just kind of vibes forecasting."*

### The Moving Target Problem
- AI is whatever machines can't do yet — once machines can do it, "that's just software"
- "That's not AI, that's just image recognition. That's not AI, that's just sentiment analysis."
- AGI is being redefined: now means "can do a certain percentage of economically valuable work"
- But an IBM mainframe in 1975 could do a meaningful percentage of economically valuable work previously done by people

### The Key Point
> *"Even if the models stopped getting better tomorrow — if this is it and we hit a brick wall tomorrow — this is an incredibly useful technology that's going to change the world and get rolled out over the next 10 years. You don't have to believe in any of that stuff to believe this is a giant deal."*

## 💡 The Coding Transformation

### The Unexpected Automation
> *"You almost didn't realize it was boring manual labor that could be automated. You thought it was something else."*

### The Expert Systems Problem with Job Predictions
> *"You can't look at a senior partner at a law firm and say, 'Well, 17% of their work could be automated.' This is horseshit."*

**Why job exposure predictions fail:**
1. You can't describe a profession by breaking it down into logical steps (the expert systems problem)
2. The taxi driver problem: in 1997, you'd say "obviously taxi drivers won't be affected by the internet" — and of course it completely changed the whole thing

**The personal trainer example:**
- "Things that won't be affected by AI: personal trainers"
- Take your iPhone, balance it with camera pointed at you, ask AI to build training routine and watch you
- Maybe complete nonsense — but that's how these things work

### The Uber vs. Airbnb Comparison
- Uber: demolished taxi business in many cities AND made the market much bigger
- Airbnb: impact on hotels is actually pretty marginal — carved out a different business
- *"My wife flies to Milwaukee next week. She wants room service, a gym at 6am, then drives to the client site. She's not going to stay in an Airbnb. Zero chance."*
- Half of hotel business is business travel — Airbnb doesn't compete there

## 📈 The TAM Expansion Point

### Marc Andreessen's Insight
- The opportunity set for companies is so much larger now
- Used to have no trillion-dollar companies; now heading toward dozens
- People haven't grasped how large companies can get

### The Platform Shift Framing
- Peak mainframe install base: ~70-80,000 units
- Internet era: 50-100 million PCs on Earth
- Today: 700-800 million consumer PCs, 5.5-6 billion smartphones
- Which is why ChatGPT can have 900 million weekly users

### The Electricity Analogy
- Electricity became part of absolutely everything
- Software has been slowly working its way out
- AI will do the same — slowly spread to do more and more things
- But: the electricity company doesn't get a percentage of your TV bill or washing machine price

## 🎯 Advice for People Worried About Their Jobs

> *"Don't stick your head in the sand and say, 'I hate all of this stuff.' That gives you a great feeling of moral superiority and you can go on Bluesky and shout at everybody about how evil AI is. Great, I'm happy for you. But that's not going to help."*

**What actually helps:**
- Dive into this completely
- Submerge yourself in it
- Come out understanding what you can do with it
- Understand how this changes things
- Understand how you can be a great hire

**The law firm example:**
- If they hired 100 associates last year and this year only 50
- Going to the interview and saying "I think AI is evil and I'm never going to use it" is probably not the right move

**The caveat:**
- On average, it'll be okay — but "on average nobody died in World War I"
- If you're a 19-year-old in 1914, you had a 1-in-3 chance of not coming back
- For some professions (associates, entry-level professional services), this is a major question with very unclear answers

## ❓ Questions Not Enough People Are Asking

1. **Do model labs actually have pricing power?** — Most people presume the current situation will continue or of course they will, without questioning it

2. **What's the task and what's the job?** — Which things become a button vs. what are people actually hiring you for?

3. **What gets unlocked that wasn't possible before?** — Not just doing the old thing but more, but what's genuinely new?
