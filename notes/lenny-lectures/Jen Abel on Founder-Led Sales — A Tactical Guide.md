*Lenny's Podcast*

## 🏗️ Why Founder-Led Sales Matters

### The Core Concept
> *"The founder is the product. When there is no brand equity, no marketing engine, limited to no referenceability — the founder is the product."*

**Three reasons founder-led sales is a competitive advantage:**
1. **The founder is the visionary** — no salesperson can speak to it like they can
2. **Highest order in the hierarchy** — the market is excited to talk to a founder because they usually know something the market doesn't
3. **The founder can see budding moments** — insights that emerge in conversations that a salesperson would miss

### The Budding Moments Insight
> *"Their day one market vision is never the same vision that takes them to product market fit. It's these little budding moments where all the gold lives."*

- Only a founder can see these moments and go deeper and deeper in conversations
- This is why founder-led sales is about learning, not just revenue

### The Game of Telephone Problem
- If a salesperson says "no one cares about this," the founder blames the salesperson
- Founder-led sales brings accountability closer to where decisions are made
- Eliminates the filter between market feedback and the person who can act on it

---

## 🚫 The Common Mistake: Hiring a Salesperson Too Early

### When NOT to Hire a Salesperson
- Before product-market fit
- Before you have data to learn from
- Before you understand who your market is
- Possibly not until Series A (seed is for experimentation, Series A is for exploiting learnings)

> *"Hiring for early stage sales — the odds are actually more against you than trying to get your next round of funding."*

### The Milestone
- Some say $500K ARR, some say $1M ARR
- If you get to $500K really fast → can move out of founder-led sales
- If you get to $500K really slowly → haven't reached velocity yet, stay in it

## 📊 The Sales Cycle: Step by Step

### The Traditional Stages
1. **Intro call** — discovery, no demo
2. **Second call** — potentially demo (depending on market)
3. **Third call** — walking through proposal/scope of work, deeper demo contextualized to learnings
4. **Fourth call** — getting feedback, co-authoring the scope of work
5. **Fifth call** — introduction to procurement
6. **Procurement** — its own mini sales cycle
7. **Post-procurement** — getting the signature, knowing who the signatory is

## 📧 Step 1: Getting Someone to Talk to You

### The Four Components of an Effective Outreach Message

**1. Relevancy** (most important)
> *"Why is this relevant for me in my role? Why are you reaching out to me?"*
- More important than personalization right now
- Personalization can come across as stressed/forced
- Tie it to where they are in their journey (recent raise, company stage, etc.)

**2. Counterintuitive or Shock Value**
> *"Say something that would literally make them say 'what?' or 'how could that be?' or 'I've never thought about it that way.'"*
- Avoid "better" — it's hard to define and means something different to everyone
- Focus on something that makes them pause
- Example from Jellyfish: *"Zero to one sales talent doesn't exist. That's why I want to have a conversation with you."*

**3. Focus on the Problem, Not the Solution**
> *"Don't even talk about the solution. Talk about the problem that you want to solve, and why it needs to be solved or why it's not good enough today."*
- Leave them wanting more
- If someone reaches out with a novel insight about a problem you're focused on, you'll probably reply

**4. Concise (No Scrolling on Mobile)**
- Three to four sentences maximum
- If they get it on their mobile phone, they shouldn't have to scroll
- That's how a founder writes, that's how an executive writes

### The Three Channels
1. **Cold email**
2. **LinkedIn DMs**
3. **Cold calling** — interest rates are often higher than email

### Tactical Test
- On Gmail, highlight the message and have it replay back to you in audio
- You'll be shocked how many notes you'll change when you hear it read back

## 📊 Conversion Rates: The Right Way to Think About Them

### Win Rate vs. Conversion Rate
- **Win rate**: If you got 10 prospects into the funnel and 1 signed, that's a 10% win rate
- If win rate is high (30-40%), you don't need as high a conversion rate on outbound
- If win rate is low, you need much higher conversion rates (more at-bats)

### Healthy Benchmarks
- Mature outbound conversion rate: ~5-7%
- Can be 2-3% or 8-15% depending on the problem and whether it's coming from the founder
- The biggest driver of conversion rate: **how well the problem is felt by the market**

> *"When a problem is truly being felt by the market, people will get on a call. People will respond."*

### The Uncomfortable Truth
- Two companies, same outreach tactics, one at 2% interest rate, one at 12%
- The only difference: the insight the founder has on the market
- *"Everyone always comes back to 'we have a sales problem.' Maybe you're just not saying anything interesting. Maybe the problem you're looking to solve just isn't widely felt."*

## 🔍 Finding the Right Leads

### Start Manual, Not with Tools
> *"Before you buy any tool, don't even think about tools. Can you manually find 30 people that you want to spend 15 to 20 minutes writing a rock solid note to?"*

**The 30-Person Exercise:**
1. Find 30 people you're deeply excited to learn from
2. Spend 15-20 minutes writing each a thoughtful note
3. Hit them on email, LinkedIn, try calling, Twitter DM — pull out all the stops
4. Track: how many respond? 1? 5? 0?

**What this exercise teaches you:**
- Are they even discoverable? (If 30 is hard, you're in trouble)
- What commonalities exist? (Team size, industry, tenure in role, previous roles)
- These commonalities become your parameters for scaling

**When to use tools like Clay:**
- Only after you've identified parameters through manual experiments
- Parameters allow you to do strong enrichment
- Without the right questions, enrichment tools yield nothing

### The Danger of Volume Too Early
- Blasting 1,000 people with Clay can damage your domain health
- Volume comes AFTER you've identified the right parameters
- *"People focus so much on volume in the beginning. Volume comes once you've identified it and know the parameters."*

## 📞 Step 2: The First Call

### Be Vulnerable and Honest
> *"I would be very open and honest with where you are. Hey, I'm an early stage startup. We are deeply passionate about solving this specific problem. We have a lot to learn."*

**Why vulnerability works:**
- If you tell them you have a fully baked product, they won't give you honest feedback
- When you're vulnerable, you get raw and honest feedback
- It's easier to tell someone "before you make this mistake, I actually don't care about that" when they haven't built it yet
- *"The further you suggest you are, you're actually going to hamstring yourself on gaining intel."*

### The Structure of the First Call
1. Open with: here's how we're thinking about the problem from a priority perspective
2. Ask: can we gain your insight into how this problem is manifesting on your side?
3. Let them open up — you're getting intel about their perception of the problem
4. Ask: tell me how this looks in your head? How are you seeing this?

### Questions to Avoid
- "What keeps you up at night?"
- "If you had a magic wand..."
- "What are your pain points today?"
- These answers change every single day

### Signs You're Onto Something
1. **Is it a growing and widening problem?** — No one spends time fixing a fixed problem
2. **Are they measuring or managing this problem?** — If no, probably not a priority
3. **How have they tried to solve it?** — Headcount? Another tool? Still an open gap?
4. **Are they bringing in colleagues?** — When they pull in other people, momentum is building

> *"When they start bringing in other colleagues, you're flipping them into a buyer."*

### End Every Call with a Booked Next Call
- Pull up calendars before getting off the call
- Ask who else should be invited
- If they say "I'll email you" — that's a leading indicator of low interest

---

## 🚫 No Demo on the First Call

> *"The demo is the only carry you control in the sales process. Once they see it, that dreaminess in their eyes — leave them wanting more."*

**Why to delay the demo:**
- Even when you do demo, don't demo everything — leave it for a second call
- Make them invest time in you first
- Upmarket (hundreds of thousands of dollars): slow down as much as possible
- Make sure all the right people are in the room before showing it
- If only one person has seen it, it's their baby — you want it to be the group's baby

**When to demo quickly:**
- Down market ($3K tool): demo as fast as possible — it's a high-volume game

## 🤝 Step 3-4: Co-Authoring the Scope

### The Power of Co-Authorship
- Turns a customer into a guide
- Helps you understand their "biomaturity" — do they even have a process to deploy this?

### The Services Insight (Controversial but Important)
> *"40 to 50% of B2B SaaS companies have to sell some form of service before they can sell a technology."*

**Why services contracts work:**
- Shows intent even if not recurring revenue
- Lets you call them a customer and use their logo
- You get paid to educate them and design their process
- Sets the mindset with the buyer around what this would look like
- Especially important for AI startups where buyers need strategy and process first

**Time-box services to 90 days:**
- So much changes beyond 90 days
- You might not want to continue either
- 90-day increments allow for scoping and reassessment

**The counter-argument to investor pushback:**
> *"Should I wait 18 months to when they're ready to buy a solution, and be the one that's not selling them because someone else educated them?"*

### When Services Are NOT Needed
- When the market already knows how to buy that technology
- When they have a process, strategy, and implementation team in place
- When you're selling down market

## 🏢 Step 5-6: Procurement

### Understanding Procurement
- Professional buyers — very smart, do this for a living
- They can suggest existing preferred vendors that do "similar-ish" work
- Deals can die because procurement suggests an existing vendor the buyer didn't even know about

### How to Win at Procurement

**1. Make it feel different, not just better**
- If it just feels slightly better, they'll suggest an existing vendor
- Must be clearly differentiated

**2. Do the work for them**
> *"Make their job easy. Literally say, 'I want to make this as easy as possible for you. Give me the forms that you need to fill out. I'll fill them out for you.'"*
- You're a small buy in a sea of large deals
- Easy to get sidelined if you don't project manage it

**3. Be precise about what you do and don't do**
- If they can't classify you, they'll send you to the "kitchen sink" MSA
- That means $5M insurance policy requirements, right to audit your books, etc.
- They classify you as high risk when they can't classify you at all

**4. Truncate contracts strategically**
- Split into a technology contract AND a service contract
- Service contract lets you onboard and prep them while IT due diligence runs
- Creates an incentive to get the technology piece through

**5. Know who the signatory is before you get there**
- CFO, CLO, business unit head, head of procurement — all possible signatories
- Prepare bullets for them in advance
- If they don't know what they're signing, you lose your queue spot and add months

### The Queue Spot Problem
- Signatories review things in order of priority
- If they kick it out for clarification, you go to the back of the queue
- Can add a month or more to your timeline

## ✍️ Step 7: Getting the Signature

### Critical Rule
> *"You do not get paid until you are approved by finance and procurement has a signature on the contract. Don't start any work."*

- Business units can't just pay you
- Payment comes through a purchase order from finance
- Don't rely on that money until the signature is on paper

## 💰 Pricing and Discounting

### When to Discount
- Only if they're doing something far beyond just using the technology
- Design partner status
- Two-year reference commitment
- Something they're giving you in return

### When NOT to Discount
> *"If I give you a 30% discount, can I remove 30% of the value? That's kind of what they're saying."*

- Don't negotiate with yourself
- "Better" is a dangerous place — ask them to define it and measure it
- Discounting just to get a deal over the line = negotiating with yourself

### ACV Sweet Spot for Early Enterprise
- $50K-$200K depending on the business unit
- For very early stage: $50K-$100K initial contract
- Deals can compound: $100K → $500K → $1M in subsequent years

## ⏱️ Sales Cycle Timeline

### What Dictates Sales Cycle Length
1. **How well you project manage it** — book next calls in a week, not two weeks
2. **Complexity of the organization** — highly regulated industries: 9-12 months minimum
3. **Budget availability** — are you creating budget or is there an existing line item?
4. **Seniority of contact** — SVP can navigate traps; director may make internal mistakes

### Typical Timelines
- Enterprise deals: 6-12 months typically
- Fast enterprise deals: 90 days (rare but possible)
- Highly regulated industries: 20-30% longer

### The Interesting Contract Clause
- Some enterprises have rules that a deal cannot exceed more than 20% of the vendor's existing revenue
- Ask if it's a hard line or negotiable

## 🎯 Market Segmentation

### Enterprise (500+ employees)
- User and buyer are different people
- Procurement involved
- Longer sales cycle but compounding returns
- Once you're in, you're in — preferred vendor status, cross-sell opportunities

### Small Business
- User and buyer are the same person
- No procurement
- Faster sales cycle
- Higher churn risk — they cancel fast and sometimes publicly
- Marketing-intensive, high-volume game

### Mid-Market (the Tricky Zone)
- Straddling two very different go-to-markets
- If it requires heavy integration, they don't have the resources
- Often outsourced to Accenture or consulting firms — creates third-party dependency
- Hard to start there

### The Key Question
> *"Which game do you want to play? And do you have an enterprise product, or do you think you're solving an enterprise problem but you're not sure yet?"*

## 🔑 The Biggest Pitfall: Qualification

> *"Everyone says they have a bottom of funnel problem. It's never a bottom of funnel problem. It's a top of funnel problem. I've actually never seen a bottom of funnel sales problem."*

**The real issues:**
- Reaching out to the wrong person
- Wrong messaging
- Not solving the right problem
- Not being different enough

**The fix:**
- Get qualification right first
- Spending time on wrong leads = zero
- All the downstream problems are symptoms of bad qualification

## 💡 Mindset Shifts for Founders Who Hate Sales

### Reframe What Sales Is
> *"If you have built something that you believe in and they have a problem — that's a beautiful thing. That's what makes the world go around."*

### The Counter-Intuitive Close
- Tell someone you don't think you're the right fit for them
- They'll try to sell you on them: "Well, what if we did this, this, and this?"
- Trust becomes the currency
- *"If you are a trusted founder, your market will continually send you leads and word of mouth."*

### Bring the Energy
> *"Sales is supposed to feel fun for the buyer. They should be like, 'This person's invigorating. They're going to change my world. They're going to get me promoted.'"*

- Some of these people are in boring jobs — give them an outlet
- Founders go stone cold face when they should bring passion
- Energy is felt by the market

## 📊 Product Market Fit Connection

### The Market-First vs. Product-First Hypothesis
- Companies that start with the market problem first (like Vanta) tend to reach PMF faster
- Companies that start with technical insight (like Airtable, Figma) have uncapped upside but are riskier
- Starting with the product is a hell of a lot riskier

### The Wiz Pattern
- Founders had 10-15 conversations a day for weeks
- Noticed shift from "I like it" to "let me pull in this person and this person"
- That shift = signal of real momentum
